# NATIONAL-LEVEL RADIO BROADCAST SCRIPT BENCHMARK

**Version:** 1.0 — produced from the 18-entry NSPC National corpus study
**Date:** 2026-08-22
**Companion artifacts:** see `national-script-analysis/` (corpus inventory, 18 per-script analyses, 13 specialist reports, 3 cross-comparison reports)

---

## Purpose

This document captures the **reusable principles** observed from National-level (NSPC) Philippine radio broadcasting scripts and is intended to guide future **Division-level** script development for our own team.

It is:

- a **benchmark, not a template** — it describes what strong National entries do, so we can understand the standard, not a fill-in-the-blanks form to imitate;
- **principles, not copied wording** — every technique is expressed as a function or structure, and all competitor-specific phrasing is explicitly quarantined in Section 21;
- **National-level inspiration, not imitation** — we study *how* the best entries achieve polish, coherence, and memorability so our own Division script can reach the same *quality*, with our own identity.

**Evidence discipline:** claims below carry labels inherited from the underlying analyses: **DIRECTLY OBSERVED** (quoted from a source script), **CROSS-SCRIPT PATTERN** (observed in multiple independent entries, counts given), and **ANALYST INFERENCE** (reasoned interpretation, flagged as such). No official NSPC judging criteria exist in the workspace; every quality judgment here is derived from the scripts themselves.

# 2. What Makes the Scripts Feel National-Level

Synthesized from the strongest recurring characteristics (all CROSS-SCRIPT PATTERN unless noted; counts from the cross-comparison report):

1. **A disciplined two-act architecture executed by a two-anchor duo.** Every entry (18/18) runs: branded open → 2–3 hard-news reports → break promise → exactly one infomercial → re-entry → sports/showbiz → recap close. The architecture is uniform; the *content inside* is where teams differentiate.
2. **Branding treated as a judged performance, not overhead.** ~17–29% of airtime (mean opening ≈ 63 s) is spent on identity: OBB, station ID, greeting, anchor self-intros, headlines. The listener's "what station is this?" is answered in the first ~20 s by the strongest entries.
3. **Fast, compressed, information-dense writing.** 3-sentence hard-news reports (mode 3, 16/41), 25–35 s reporter blocks, 8–14-word headlines with result-state verbs, statute-level legal precision in the crime story (13/18), "Ayon sa…" attribution in the second breath (11/18).
4. **Sound marks structure, not sentences.** Category-differentiated music beds (18/18), beds ducking under speech ("UP AND UNDER", ≥16/18), stingers only at boundaries, one audio cue ≈ every 15 s at rest.
5. **The infomercial as a dramatized mid-show pivot.** Exactly one, mid-broadcast (~44–58% through), 35–65 s, dramatized as a scene or game (17/18) rather than a lecture, bookended by a return promise and a re-entry.
6. **Chemistry manufactured in the script.** Question-form reporter handoffs (12/18), thank-you receipts (11/18), unison "money lines" at open/close (18/18), paired self-intros (17/18), shared-sentence completion (6/18), reaction tokens (6/18). The scripts *write the spontaneity*.
7. **Reporter sign-off discipline.** 32 of 34 reporter-delivered reports end with a named sign-off carrying station/beat identity.
8. **A closing ritual that completes the loop.** Recap line → anchor IDs → station identity/slogan → music fade (18/18), median ≈ 19 s.
9. **A central concept when present is a *system*, not a line.** Only 2/18 entries (C5 travel, C8 digital) carry a complete concept system; the rule is "concept paid off at least twice" — a one-off slogan reads as decoration.
10. **Execution cleanliness is the widest quality gap — bigger than any structural difference.** The corpus's worst recurring flaw is delivery: 16/18 entries garble lines on air, 8/18 mispronounce their own program name. The top-ranked entries (C16, C10) are the cleanest *speakers*; the bottom-ranked (C2, C12) are the most garbled. (ANALYST INFERENCE, supported by the quality ranking)

# 3. Common Broadcast Architecture

Generalized from 18/18 rundown tables (DIRECTLY OBSERVED; classification per cross-comparison counts):

```
SONIC HOOK (OBB/theme/jingle)                        [COMMON — 18/18]
↓
STATION ID (slogan → callsign+freq → program name)   [COMMON — 18/18; within first ~20 s in strongest entries]
↓
ANCHOR GREETING (+ date check; + optional time check)[COMMON — 18/18; time check 12/18; Bisaya line 10/18]
↓
ANCHOR SELF-INTRODUCTION (paired)                    [COMMON — 17/18]
↓
HEADLINES (2–4 items, stingered, alternating)        [COMMON — 18/18; 2-item 9/18, 3-item 3/18, 4-item 6/18]
↓
HARD NEWS (2–3 reports: local/national/world)        [COMMON — 18/18; 2 stories 13/18, 3 stories 5/18]
↓
BREAK TEASER + RETURN PROMISE                        [COMMON — 17/18; "magbabalik" family 14/18]
↓
INFOMERCIAL / PSA (exactly one)                      [COMMON — 18/18; 35–65 s, mean ≈ 48 s]
↓
RE-ENTRY (station ID and/or time check)              [COMMON — 13/18 spoken; +3 audio-only]
↓
SPORTS (optional)                                    [OPTIONAL — 14/18; absent C6, C12, C13, C16]
↓
SHOWBIZ (optional)                                   [OPTIONAL — 17/18; absent C7]
↓
CLOSING: recap → anchor IDs → station ID/slogan → fade [COMMON — 18/18; 12–33 s, median ≈ 19 s]
```

**Universal structure (18/18):** branded open → ≥2 hard-news reports → exactly one mid-broadcast infomercial → post-break soft block → recap close. **Flexible elements:** opening architecture (ID-first 12/18 vs headlines-first 4/18 vs blended 2/18), news ordering (national-first 13/18 vs local-first 4/18 vs none 1/18), sports/showbiz order (sports→showbiz 10/13 vs showbiz→sports 3/13), time-check placement (open vs post-break), close length (12–33 s). **Notable exceptions:** C16 (no sports, slowest open 88 s — ranked #1), C18 (11 segments incl. editorial, most fragmented delivery), C7 (no showbiz, serious throughout), C5 (complete travel concept, no spoken break teaser), C12 (no break promise at all — bottom-ranked).

**Implication for us (ANALYST INFERENCE):** following this skeleton is *expected* at any level; it will not differentiate. Differentiation comes from execution (Section 20) and from the optional layers (concept system, chemistry devices, clean delivery).

# 4. Opening Standard

Observed best practices (from Specialist B's 18-entry first-30-seconds comparison):

**First 5 seconds:** a sonic hook + the first identity statement. The strongest entries brand on line one (C1 ID voice, C4 jingle, C16 time-check naming the program). A pure-music runway (C11's 17 s) or unlabeled bed (C12) is a failure signature. (DIRECTLY OBSERVED)

**First 15 seconds:** complete the station ID. The winning pattern is a **three-layer ID build: promise/slogan → callsign+frequency → program name**, each on its own audio beat (C4, C13, C14, C16 are the models). Every opening with branding latency ≥ 28 s (C2, C9, C11, C13, C18) is also among the weakest; every top-ranked opening brands within 20 s. (CROSS-SCRIPT PATTERN + ANALYST INFERENCE)

**First 30 seconds:** greeting (optionally with a Bisaya/local line), date check, anchor self-intros as **name + role** ("inyong tagapagbalita", "inyong travel buddy" — C5, C7, C10, C16, C17, C18) or as a split joint ID (C16's "Kami ang inyong kagapay: Louie de Vera… / …at Lian Calmoring!"), then a **hook toward the news**: a question (C1 "MJ, ano bang nakahain ngayong araw?"), a chant (C13 "Heto na!"), or a time check (C16, C18). (DIRECTLY OBSERVED)

**Energy and anticipation:** the open must be *filled with identity beats*, not dead air. C4's 71 s open works because every second carries a beat; C12's open fails because two good lines are separated by ~20 s of music. The corpus's two slowest-to-news openings (C16 88 s, C5 85 s) are both top-tier — slowness is survivable only when the material is charming and complete. (ANALYST INFERENCE)

**Concept reveal:** when an entry has a central concept, it is announced inside the first ~45 s and paid off later (C5 travel, C8 digital, C17 slogan, C6 tandem). A concept introduced and abandoned (C4's "walang pinapanigan" ×3 in one minute) reads as padding. (DIRECTLY OBSERVED + ANALYST INFERENCE)

# 5. Branding Standard

- **Callsign/frequency/program title:** invented station identity is the corpus norm (18/18 "…Patrol" frame; 14/18 add an invented callsign + frequency, ~13 reusing a "91.26" pattern — a shared creative prop, not real frequencies). Our own names must be invented, never reused. (DIRECTLY OBSERVED)
- **Consistency is the brand.** The program title is the most repeated token; entries that say "NSPC Patrol" correctly every time (C1, C10, C13, C14, C16) all rank top-half; entries with brand drift (C2 "NSBC" ×3, C9, C11 "MSPC/LSPC", C12 "NSP, si Patron", C15 "NSBC!") all rank bottom-half. (CROSS-SCRIPT PATTERN + ANALYST INFERENCE)
- **One repeatable slogan, scheduled across the broadcast** — opening → one mid-show beat (bumper or sign-off) → close. C17's "Tatak-totoo!" system (ID + 3 reporter sign-offs + close) is the model; C4's triple-in-one-minute is the anti-model. Fix the exact form before rehearsal — C17's totoo→tutok→patrol drift shows improvisation fragments the brand. (DIRECTLY OBSERVED)
- **One sonic logo, used sparingly.** Only C4 (frequency sweep), C5 (car horn "Pipip!"), C15 (sung theme) own a specific, repeatable sound; generic `[STATION STINGER]` labels (~13/18) are punctuation, not identity. Use a logo at open and close only. (ANALYST INFERENCE)
- **The truth/service register is the genre default** ("katotohanan", "serbisyo", "walang pinapanigan" — 8+ entries). It cannot differentiate; differentiation comes from the delivery system around it. (CROSS-SCRIPT PATTERN + ANALYST INFERENCE)
- **Repetition limits:** a slogan heard at open, mid, and close is a system; the same line 3× in one minute is padding; 5+ platform mentions in 5 minutes (C8) is near the annoyance ceiling. (ANALYST INFERENCE)

# 6. Anchor Standard

From Specialist C's 18-entry chemistry analysis:

**Structure:** every entry uses a two-anchor duo (18/18) — lead + co-anchor — who bookend the show, chain headlines, and own transitions. No solo-anchor broadcast exists in the corpus.

**Role division:** Anchor 1 typically leads (first headline, final sign-off); Anchor 2 typically owns the second greeting (often Bisaya), a second headline, one category lead (usually showbiz), and half the close. C16 is the model balance: each anchor owns ≥1 full segment lead, ≥1 toss, and half the closing build. (CROSS-SCRIPT PATTERN)

**Recurring interaction patterns (reusable, in abstract):**
1. **Paired self-intro** — "Ako si X… / At ako naman si Y." (17/18)
2. **Name-question toss** — "[Reporter], ano ang…?" (12/18; canonical showbiz variant "ano ang chika?" in 5/18)
3. **Thank-you receipt** — "Maraming salamat, [Name]!" closes every reporter loop (11/18)
4. **Unison "money line"** at the ID and sign-off (18/18) — synchronized delivery is the cheapest chemistry proof
5. **Shared-sentence completion** — each half a complete breath unit (6/18: C1, C3, C5, C9, C16, C17); works only when halves are 2–4-word semantic beats; chopped mid-phrase fragmentation (C18) reads as hesitation
6. **Reaction tokens** between headlines — "Naku, problema 'to!" (6/18) — manufactured spontaneity; keep tokens 2–6 words
7. **"kaya" speculative questions** — "ano kaya ang epekto nito?" (7/18) — curiosity engines; the segment answers the question the audience now wants answered
8. **Mid-report follow-up Q&A** — one pre-answerable status question per broadcast ("Nasaan na ngayon ang suspek?") simulates live two-way (8/18)

**Chemistry tiers (ANALYST INFERENCE):** Partners (C1, C4, C5, C6, C13, C16 — split sentences, Q&A banter, shared catchphrases) > Hosts (C8, C14, C15, C17 — conversational, asymmetric) > Readers (C2, C3, C7, C9, C11, C12 — functional handoffs, zero interpersonal layer) > Commentator-led (C10, C18 — one voice dominates). Rhythm comes from **alternation of intent** (question/answer, setup/land, start/finish), not just alternation of turns.

**Audience engagement:** inclusive plural address is universal ("mga kababayan", "partners", "mga kapatrol", "kabiyahero"); "po" politeness in anchor frame lines; service reminders embedded in banter (C4 "stay hydrated", C6 heat index). (CROSS-SCRIPT PATTERN)

**Name discipline (critical):** anchor self-name slips at transitions (C1, C2, C4, C7) and reporter-name mismatches (C3, C4, C12, C14, C15) are the highest-frequency credibility risks. Every toss line and thank-you must be proofread for cross-names.

# 7. Headline Standard

From Specialist D's reverse-engineering of all 18 rundowns:

**Form:** `[TOPIC NOUN PHRASE], [PASSIVE RESULT VERB / RESULT STATE]!` — 8–14 words, one breath, result verb front-loaded ("arestado", "timbog", "naudlot", "umakyat", "nakumpiska"). 17/18 use this shape; exclamation is the default terminal punctuation (delivery instruction, not decoration). (CROSS-SCRIPT PATTERN)

**Block architecture:** frame line → 2–4 items with per-item audio separator (16/18) → handoff line. Frame ("Sa ratsada ng mga balita:") and handoff ("Simulan na ang balitaan!") turn a staccato list into a program (7/18 with both — C4, C5, C10, C16, C17 are the models). Hard news first, soft categories last, anchors alternating. (CROSS-SCRIPT PATTERN)

**Tease strategy — the corpus's cleanest rule: reveal the hard, tease the soft.** Hard-news headlines state the outcome; sports/showbiz headlines may use question-form with "kaya" ("sino-sinong artista kaya ang wagi sa Oscars?"). A question-form hard-news tease appears zero times. (CROSS-SCRIPT PATTERN)

**Weakness modes to avoid:** fragments posing as headlines (C2 "At impormasyon."), same story twice in one rundown (C6), content-free teases naming nothing (C14's "highlights na resulta…"), 18–20-word headlines (C17), density without air (C18's 3 headlines in ~10 s).

**Abstract formulas (reusable):**
- `[TOPIC], [RESULT STATE]!` — hard-news workhorse
- `[WARNING ADJ] + [TOPIC] + dahil sa [CAUSE]!` — crisis lead item
- `[QUESTION] + [kaya]?` — sports/showbiz tease only
- `[IMPERATIVE: Alamin/Tutukan/Abangan] + [OBJECT]` — follow-up items
- `[EVENT RESULT]! [NAMED FILIPINO], [STATE]!` — sports compound headline
- `[FRAME] + (HEADLINE + STINGER) × 2–4 + [HANDOFF]` — the block itself

# 8. Hard-News Standard

From Specialist E's inventory of all 41 hard-news reports:

**The five load-bearing slots (38/41 reports):**

```
LEAD → SOURCE → ESSENTIAL DETAIL → IMPACT → SIGN-OFF
```

With optional **anchor context/hook** before and **Q&A extension** after (8 reports). The invariant ordering: **event → identity → legal/technical frame → consequence → public advisory**. (CROSS-SCRIPT PATTERN)

**Leads:** the strongest open with a passive action verb + actor attached ("Timbog sa ikinasang operasyon ng Ormoc City Cyber Response Team…", "Nakumpiska ng mga sundalong Pilipino ang sampung bote ng cyanide…"). Data-first works for the oil story ("Sumirit na sa 43%…"). Fragments and warning-first leads are the weak class (C5, C9, C11). (DIRECTLY OBSERVED + ANALYST INFERENCE)

**Length and density:** 3 sentences is the norm (mode 3, 16/41); 25–35 s of uninterrupted reporter speech; 2–3 facts per sentence is the spoken-Filipino ceiling (C10's ~4.5 is the impressive outlier). One statute cited in a clause (RA 12010 / Cybercrime Prevention Act — 13/18) reads as researched. (CROSS-SCRIPT PATTERN)

**Attribution and hedging:** "Ayon sa…" in the second breath (11/18 name an agency or official); named persons (Trump, an NBI official) are the clearest marker of script maturity (8 reports); allegations carry "umano/diumano/daw" (C1, C8, C17, C18 — correct; C5's unhedged "sabotahe" is the corpus's worst sourcing lapse). 8/41 reports carry no attribution — a failure class. (CROSS-SCRIPT PATTERN)

**Fact discipline:** the shared news pool produces cross-entry contradictions (suspect age 20/29/binatilyo; $3.98/gal vs $70/gal; fire vs flood at Fujairah). Lesson: keep a fact sheet, pick one internally consistent version of every number and name. (ANALYST INFERENCE from DIRECTLY OBSERVED contradictions)

**Report endings:** close with the public advisory ("Hinihikayat ng awtoridad ang publiko na mag-report…") before the sign-off — the listener gets a takeaway before the name (~10/18). (CROSS-SCRIPT PATTERN)

# 9. Reporter Handoff Standard

Strong handoff patterns (all reusable in abstract):

1. **Summary lead + name + question** — the strongest family: anchor compresses the story, names the reporter, asks a pre-answerable question ("[Name], ano ang pinakahuling kaganapan d'yan?"). The question pre-loads the reporter's first sentence. (12/18)
2. **"Narito si X" / "Nakatutok si X"** — situation → name; the "nakatutok" (tuned-in) variant adds live urgency. (18/18 use ≥1 handoff formula)
3. **Location/live frame** — "Live sa Ormoc City, si Mica Ruor." justifies the voice change and sells the field patch. (C2, C7, C13, C14)
4. **Thank-you receipt after sign-off** — closes the two-voice loop and gives the anchor a beat to re-take the mic; its absence (C10, C18 local) makes returns abrupt. (11/18)
5. **Mid-report follow-up question** — one per broadcast, on status ("Nasaan na ngayon ang suspek?"), both halves written on the same page. (8/18)

**Failure modes:** name-call alone without a summary lead (C4's "Ramel!" false-start pileup), self-name slips in the toss (C1 "…MJ!"), name mismatches (C9 reporter answers the wrong anchor), cold reporter self-starts with no anchor frame (C8 sports, C16/C18 showbiz).

# 10. Reporter Sign-Off Standard

32 of 34 reporter-delivered reports end with a named sign-off (NEAR-UNIVERSAL). Formula families (reusable in abstract):

1. **Name + station/program** — the safe default: "[Name], ulat sa [Program]." (most common)
2. **Name + beat persona** — "Nakatutok sa balita, [Name]!" / "Nagpapatrol, [Name]" — re-anchors the segment role
3. **Name + station tagline fusion** — "[Name], [Slogan]!" (C17's "Tatak-totoo") — turns the sign-off into branding
4. **Category-variant family under one house formula** — the model is C10's one-word swap: news "nagmamasid sa mga balitang napapanahon" → showbiz "nagmamasid sa mga chika na napapanahon". Genre shift costs one word when the formula is strong.

**Discipline rules:** every reporter signs off, every time (C7's nameless "Live mula sa Ormoc City!" and C16's missing national sign-off are pattern breaks); sign-off lines are the most-garbled recurring line in the corpus — they are the most rehearsable lines in radio, so their failure signals under-preparation (Specialist M).

# 11. Transition Standard

From Specialist F's 18-entry seam catalog. **What makes transitions feel continuous rather than segmented:**

1. **The three-layer seam** (strongest entries): spoken line (why) + stinger/bed change (what changed) + identity (who we are). C7's return (full ID + stinger + jingle bed) is the cleanest re-anchor in the corpus.
2. **Question bridges outperform statement bridges** for handoffs and category changes — the question pre-loads the next voice.
3. **Tease-then-promise beats promise-only at the break** — the promise says "we'll be back"; the tease says "you should want us back" (C1, C16, C13, C9, C4 vs the promise-only entries).
4. **One-word connectors that trust the bed** — "Samantala", "Sa ibang balita" + a bed change is all the corpus needs; the music does the cognitive work.
5. **The return must re-state identity** — 13/18 do it; the 5 that don't (C5, C8, C12, C16, partially C18) have the least anchored post-break minutes.
6. **Transition functions taxonomy (reusable):** RESET (mark a new block: bed swap + accent stinger; launch line), TRANSFER (hand the mic: summary+name+question; live frame), HOLD (through the break: tease-then-promise; "after this paalala" frame), RE-ANCHOR (after the break: ID voice; time check; catchphrase), SHIFT TONE (category change: label + bed swap; curiosity bridge; tonal hook), CLOSE (recap + outro bed + ensemble chant + theme callback).

**Critical:** transition lines are the highest-risk lines in the script — short, unquotable, easy to fumble. C2/C3/C9/C11's garbled seams and C1/C4's name slips at exactly the re-orientation points are the corpus's clearest "written but not delivered" failures. Rehearse transitions like the opening.

# 12. Sound-Design Standard

From Specialist G's cue-label analysis of all 18 entries. Principles:

### USE
- **Category-differentiated music beds** — news/sports/showbiz/infomercial/closing each get their own bed (18/18); the listener "hears" the genre before a word is spoken. Keep the map small: **4–5 beds maximum** for a student operator (8–11 beds, as in C13/C18, overloads one person).
- **"UP AND UNDER" / "DUCKS UNDER SPEECH" notation** on every bed change (≥16/18) — the corpus's most repeated production phrase and its primary anti-crowding mechanism.
- **Boundary-only stingers** — one per headline, one per segment change, one at break-in, one at return; withheld inside reporter blocks (reporter blocks run 20–40 s with zero internal hits). ~1 cue per 15–25 s is the proven rate.
- **Sonic bookend** — same ID/chorus at open and close (13/18), plus one re-entry stinger after the break.
- **Crescendo-and-fade ending** — the universal completion signal (fade 18/18; explicit swell-to-climax ~8/18).
- **One diegetic realism cue** — a time-check beep (8/18) or phone-filter on a "live" report (C2, C7); use exactly one, never repeat it.
- **Self-describing cue labels** — "[SHOWBIZ SPARKLE / GLITZ ACCENT]" (C14) beats "[STINGER]"; the operator should never guess which file to play.

### USE SPARINGLY
- Vocal/unison stingers ("Offline!", "Tumpak!", "Eksklusibo!") — zero-cost branding, one per broadcast.
- Genre accent SFX (whistle for sports, sparkle for showbiz — C14 is the only clean example) — culturally legible iconography only.
- Scripted silence — one comic beat (C2's classroom silence) or one dramatic pause inside an ID (C7) is powerful; more than that reads as dead air.
- Theatrical SFX — confine them to the infomercial zone (C1 game show, C2 bell, C3 keyboard/thunder/evil laugh, C18 quiz jingle); this contrast is what makes the break feel like a different program.

### AVOID
- **Density above ~3 SFX/min** — C2 and C3 (3.6/min) are also the corpus's most garbled entries; sound clutter competes with dialogue instead of punctuating it.
- **Generic stinger labels** with no sound identity (~12/18 use them) — they leave operators guessing and give the station no sonic signature.
- **Stinger stacking** — two impacts within one second (C7's open) reads as a technical flub.
- **Ambience under dialogue** — C5's traffic bed is the exception that proves the "ducks under speech" rule.
- **Music-only gaps** — a bed without dialogue does not carry a segment (C2's ~13 s dead air, C12's ~50 s near-empty infomercial window, C16's 26 s music-only stretch are all flagged failures).

# 13. Infomercial Standard

From Specialist H's 18-entry comparison. The canonical skeleton (17/18 intelligible entries):

```
ENTRY BUMPER (return promise + tease)
→ HOOK (sound or question, ~5 s)
→ CHARACTER/SCENARIO (3-voice dramatization, 10-20 s)
→ PROBLEM/CONFLICT (personal stakes first)
→ INFORMATION (fact; the weakest universal step)
→ SOLUTION (named program beats abstraction)
→ CALL TO ACTION (verb + object + named entity)
→ SLOGAN/TAGLINE (custom slogan or station-attribution tag)
→ RETURN STINGER
```

**Corpus evidence for each step:** entry promise 16/18 ("Huwag kayong aalis, kami ay magbabalik!"); dramatized format 17/18 (skit 12/18, game show 2/18, chant 1/18); the recurring cast is **adult authority + struggling child + narrator** (7/18); duration mean ≈ 48 s, median band 40–50 s, placed at ~44–58% of the broadcast; the station-attribution tag ("Ang paalalang ito ay hatid sa inyo ng himpilang ito") is a real-radio convention (5/18).

**The differentiating variables:**
- **Fact → named solution → named CTA chain** (C13's model: "30 to 40 percent… nag-improve dahil sa ARAL Program" → "sama-sama nating suportahan ang DepEd ARAL Program"; C7's "I-report ang mga paglabag sa Animal Welfare Act!" is the strongest CTA).
- **Weak/absent CTA is the corpus's most common advocacy flaw (9/18)** — a PSA without a "do X" is a mood piece.
- **Statistic hygiene:** only 5/18 name a source; mangled numbers (C4's "1%" inversion, C7's missing numerator) are the most damaging content-level errors.
- **Education/literacy dominates (9/18)** — choosing a different, credible topic (or a distinctive execution of education) differentiates.

**Taste boundaries observed:** shaming children (C2, C6, C13) and the dog-eating joke (C10) are flagged risks; C18's self-deprecating wrong answers are the model of safe comedy. Keep the break 40–50 s; 64–69 s (C7, C8) crowds the news.

# 14. Sports Standard

From Specialist I's 14-entry sports analysis:

- **Register:** sports is written in **news register with concerned energy** — not hype. The shared story (a stranded Filipino athlete) is a human-interest news item: LEAD → CAUSE → RESPONSE → SIGN-OFF, with agency framing (PSC monitoring/advisory).
- **Energy comes from the bed, not the vocabulary** — sports beds are the fastest, most percussive in the corpus ("HIGH ENERGY ROCK", "FAST SPORTS DRUM BED"); sports slang is nearly absent ("sports timeout" C5 and "rain or shine" C8 are the only overt play).
- **Anchor intros lean emotional-involved:** "Nakakapanghinayang na balita naman para sa mga fans…" (C3), "Kumusta na kaya ang mga atletang apektado…?" (C14) — sympathy, not celebration.
- **Reporter identity is functional, not persona:** "Sports Patroller", "sports authority" — a job title, not a character.
- **Sign-off: category-variant family** under the house formula (C1's "inyong Sports Patroller!"; C17's "tatak-tutok!" carries the station slogan in).
- **Best practice:** tease the sports story before the break, deliver it after (C4); carry the station slogan into the sports sign-off (C17, C14).
- **Absence is viable:** 4 of 18 entries skip sports entirely (including #1-ranked C16) — they substitute a news story or longer close. If we drop a category, substitute deliberately.

# 15. Showbiz Standard

From Specialist I's 17-entry showbiz analysis:

- **Tone shift via lexicon:** the "chika/tsikahan" cluster is the single most reliable genre switch (10/18) — it renegotiates the reporter–audience contract from informer to companion. It is never used in sports or hard news. (A Division script should invent or adopt its own genre word if desired — "chika" itself risks reading as the default.)
- **Tone shift via sound:** distinct glamorous/upbeat bed + category accent (C14's sparkle), entered through the station's own stinger so identity survives the playfulness.
- **Tone shift via persona:** named showbiz personas (C8 "Yas Queen", C15 "Mamshie Kirby", C12 "Chika-Dora") carry the tone and survive delivery slips better than scripted punchlines. Persona intensity correlates with segment quality; generic reporters produce the flattest segments.
- **Humor is persona-based, not joke-based** — "the joke is who is talking." Humor lives almost exclusively in showbiz (and the infomercial); sports stays earnest.
- **Formula preservation is the ideal:** C10's one-word swap (news sign-off → "nagmamasid sa mga chika na napapanahon") proves the genre shift can cost exactly one word; C6/C11's unchanged news sign-off flattens the category.
- **Identity preservation:** the station slogan rides into the soft sign-off (C17 "Tatak-totoo!", C14 "NSPC Patrol!"); the "…Patrol" naming system absorbs the categories ("Showbiz Patroller").
- **Placement:** showbiz last (12/17) creates the natural comedown before the formal sign-off; then the closing spiel can swing back to solemn without whiplash.
- **Warning:** showbiz garbling is often the last thing a judge hears before the sign-off — the C15 Michael-Jordan/NBA error and C8's garbled Yas Queen closer are higher-cost than mid-news slips. Also: anchor lead-in matters — cold reporter self-starts (C16, C18) weaken integration.

# 16. Closing Standard

From Specialist A's closing-length data and Specialist F's close taxonomy:

**Recurring anatomy (18/18):** recap line → anchor self-identification → station identity/slogan (often ensemble/unison) → outro music swell and fade. Duration 12–33 s (median ≈ 19 s) — roughly one-third to one-quarter of the opening's length.

**Recap line family:** "At iyan po ang mga balitang inyong tinutukan ngayong araw!" (C1, C4, C8, C10, C11, C15, C16, C17, C18) — the "we're done" cue. (Do not copy the line; the *function* is a one-line recap before identity.)

**Completion devices:**
- **Unison station chant** with the full team (C1, C7, C10, C11, C12, C15, C17, C18) — the branding peak.
- **Theme callback** — a closing line that only makes sense if you heard the opening (C5 "Nakarating na ang ating biyahe", C8 "Offline!", C10/C6 "Magpadayon!") — the difference between a sign-off and a *signature*.
- **Call-and-response moral stack** — C16's parallel "hindi sapat ang makinig… / …maging mapanuri!" chain is the corpus's most structured closing device.
- **Extensions (optional):** moral spiel (C3, C5, C6, C10, C13, C16, C17), editorial (C18 — the only dedicated editorial segment), banter/reminder (C4), digital plug (C8), TD credit (C18).
- **Crescendo-and-fade** under the closing lines — the anchors sign off over a rising bed; the fade to silence is itself the designed end.

**Failure modes:** a generic recap naming no story; a garbled final ID (C15 "ABCJP 9126, NSBC!"); an emotionally strong close that abandons brand duty (C3, C6 — no station name in the final 90 s); a closing chant with no in-show referent (C15's "Ang kikisig! Husto! Aksyon!").

# 17. Language Standard

From Specialist J's language analysis:

- **Base: Filipino/Taglish** (18/18); English occupies five predictable slots — numbers, proper nouns/institutions, showbiz/sports slang, Filipino-affixed English verbs ("na-stranded", "i-cancel"), and social address ("Mare", "partner"). Optional Bisaya/Cebuano line as local color (10/18).
- **Three-register architecture:** formal news body (reporters) ↔ conversational-energetic anchor frame (greeting, tease, handoff, close) ↔ slang-heavy showbiz. The shift is cued by vocabulary (street "timbog" for the arrest, legal "RA 12010" for the consequence — a deliberate juxtaposition), not grammar alone. Keep formal grammar confined to the story body.
- **The gloss device (high value, only 3/18 use it):** "cyanide o nakalalasong kemikal", "inflation o bilis ng pagtaas sa presyo ng mga bilihin", "pagsulay o pagsubok" — preserves the term, guarantees comprehension, gives the talent a recoverable fallback if the English word slips.
- **Verbal rhythm devices, placed at the edges:** alliteration in slogans and sign-offs ("mapanuri/mapanindigan/mapanagutan", "bumabalita para sa bayan"); rhyme only on naturally-phrased parallel lines (forced-rhyme inversion, C14's "pagtasyon", was garbled in delivery — rhyme is the highest-risk device); one-word punchlines as terminal landing beats ("Tumpak!", "Eksklusibo!", "Offline!" — open-vowel finals land cleanly); reduplication and chant bookends ("Heto na! Heto na!", "tuloy-tuloy").
- **Pronunciation-friendly writing is identifiable and measurable:** short declaratives, one fact per clause, one number per clause, fronted predicates, parallelism. The cleanest scripts (C16 full-sentence; C10 long-formal) succeed on one main clause per sentence with a clear S-V-O spine; the most garbled (C2, C12, C13) stack subordinate clauses or fragment into 1–2-word lines.
- **The mispronunciation cluster is consistent:** English proper nouns (Fujairah ~10 entries, Strait of Hormuz ~6, A24, AFASA, Chalamet, "Brent crude" → "breadfruit oil"). Cap unfamiliar English tokens at one per sentence, gloss them, or verbalize numbers in Filipino ("higit isang daan at labing-isang dolyar kada bariles" — C6's listener-friendly practice).
- **"po" politeness** marks the anchor frame lines; banter drops it. Exclamations and reaction tokens ("Naku!", "Aba d'yan!") punctuate the conversational tier.

**Airtime split (ANALYST INFERENCE):** ~25% openers/transitions, ~30% hard news, ~45% soft (advocacy + sports + showbiz + closing). The "news program" is really a variety program — each genre gets one 30–50 s slot and must be entered and exited quickly.

**Typical National pacing profile (5:00):** OBB/ID 20–40 s → greeting/date 15–40 s → headlines 10–30 s → first news ~1:05 → news spine to ~2:30 → break teaser ~2:20–2:45 → infomercial ~2:35–3:25 → re-entry ~3:25–3:35 → sports ~3:35–4:15 → showbiz ~4:00–4:40 → close ~4:35–5:00.

**Rhythm rules:** anchor-pair alternation every 2–5 s in openers (the fastest-feeling zone); reporter monologues as the slow islands; ~1 audio cue/15 s at rest, 2–3× hotter in openings and transitions, withheld during monologues; ~13 turns/min + ~4 cues/min ≈ 14–17 attention resets/min at rest (20–42 in ultra-fragmented entries, 8–12 in block-style entries).

**Both extremes carry risk:** sub-3 s average turns (C2, C4, C6, C18) risk breathlessness; 30+ s monologues (C10's 38 s) risk losing the listener. Target the balanced newsroom band: ~3–8 s turns, 25–35 s reporter blocks, one Q&A to break up the news spine.

# 19. Concept & Originality Standard

From Specialist L's system-vs-gimmick framework:

**What separates a complete concept system from an intro gimmick** (ANALYST INFERENCE, evidenced by C5/C8 vs C4/C7/C15):

| Criterion | Complete system (C5 travel, C8 digital) | Intro gimmick |
|---|---|---|
| Vocabulary range | Concept words at open, transitions, sign-offs, close (C5: 10+ instances across 5 positions) | Concept confined to opening (C4: 3× in 60 s, 0 after) or close (C15 chant) |
| Closing answers opening | "Offline!" inverts "Tama muna pag-scroll"; "Nakarating na ang ating biyahe" completes the departure | Close introduces a *new* image (C7) or an unexplained shout (C15) |
| Sound belongs to the concept | Car horn (C5); drum roll + unison "Offline!" (C8) | No sonic identity, or chant with no referent (C15) |
| Break/infomercial integrated | Red-light stop inside the trip (C5); app plugs inside news handoffs (C8) | Infomercial is a separate play (C4, C6, C7) |
| Repetition discipline | Each device used ≤ its function (horn once) | Same slogan 3× in one minute (C4) |
| Consistency of form | Same referent every time | Variant slips fragment the brand (C17 totoo/tutok/patrol) |

**One-line rule:** *A concept is a system when it gives you language for the whole broadcast and a sound for its edges; it is a gimmick when it gives you one good line for the first 30 seconds — or a shout for the last five.*

**Key facts:** only 2/18 entries are complete systems; both are top-half but not #1/#2 — a complete concept is *not required to win* (C16's concept lives in the close; C10's is a sign-off formula). What is required: **a concept, once introduced, is paid off at least twice**. One-off catchphrases (C4 "Ulam na!", C7 "Ang totoong boses ng Ormoc") are broken promises. The corpus ceiling: "concept as container," not "concept as content" — no entry organizes its news itself through the concept.

**Gimmick-risk ranking (highest → lowest):** C15 close chant > C12 persona puns > C6 katandem density > C13 stacked open > C8 platform plugs > C4 slogan triple > C5 (lowest — the metaphor does structural work, the horn is used once).

# 20. National-Level Techniques Worth Adopting

Ranked (from the cross-comparison reusable-techniques catalog; ratings framed for a Division-level team with no expensive production tools):

### HIGH PRIORITY (strongly improve quality; mostly EASY)
1. **Identity consistency discipline** — say your own station/program/anchor names right, every time; proofread every toss and thank-you. The highest-return fix in the corpus (brand-stable entries dominate the ranking). EASY.
2. **Action-first leads with the actor attached** — never open a hard-news report with a fragment. EASY.
3. **3-sentence, 25–35 s reports** with "Ayon sa…" in the second breath and a public advisory before the sign-off. EASY–MODERATE.
4. **The state-change headline formula** — `[TOPIC], [RESULT STATE]!`, 8–14 words, one per category, stinger per item. EASY.
5. **Reveal hard news, tease soft news** — question-form "kaya" teases only for sports/showbiz. EASY.
6. **The handoff loop** — name-question toss + thank-you receipt for every reporter; one mid-report follow-up Q&A. EASY.
7. **Category-variant sign-off formula** (the one-word swap, C10-style) under one house skeleton. EASY.
8. **Category beds + "UP AND UNDER" notation** — 4–5 beds max, stingers at boundaries only, ~1 cue/15–25 s. MODERATE (operator).
9. **Tease-then-promise break loop + re-entry with station identity** — never enter or leave a break without both. EASY.
10. **The "X o Y" gloss** for hard words + verbalized numbers + cap English tokens at one per sentence. EASY — the cheapest delivery insurance.
11. **First news at ~55–65 s**; rehearsal to a stopwatch for the ~5:00 window. EASY.
12. **One unison "money line" at open and one at close.** EASY.
13. **Fact → named solution → named CTA in the infomercial** — one verified statistic, one named entity, one verb-object ask. EASY.
14. **Post-break re-entry identity + time check** as the return ritual. EASY.

### MEDIUM PRIORITY (useful; dependent on concept/execution)
15. **Shared-sentence completion** (2–3 split lines per broadcast: self-intro, break bumper, close) — MODERATE: a fumbled split is worse than none.
16. **Reaction tokens between headlines** — EASY, but tie each to the other anchor's content; don't stack three identical.
17. **Slogan system on a schedule** (open → mid → close; fixed form) — MODERATE (discipline).
18. **Three-layer station ID inside 20 s** (promise → callsign+freq → program name) — EASY.
19. **Dramatized 3-voice infomercial** with hook sound + comic beat, 40–50 s — MODERATE (three castable voices).
20. **Persona-based soft-segment reporter** with one catchphrase, contained to the showbiz slot — MODERATE (performance).
21. **Mid-report simulated live two-way** (one Q&A) — MODERATE (airtight cueing).
22. **A closing that answers the opening** (theme callback) — EASY, once a concept exists.
23. **Recap + anchor IDs + station identity + fade closing ritual** — EASY.
24. **One diegetic realism cue** (time-check beep) — EASY.
25. **Local-angle reframe of international stories** ("ano ang ibig sabihin nito para sa atin?") — EASY.
26. **"Samantala"/one-word connectors + bed change between stories** — EASY (needs the bed map).

### OPTIONAL (creative choices, not necessities)
27. **Complete concept system** (vocabulary + sound + transitions + break integration + closing loop) — DIFFICULT; only 2/18 at National did it; a simplified bookended version is MODERATE.
28. **Game-show infomercial** — high yield but already a shared template (2/18); only if our concept supports it.
29. **Generative brand prefix** (C17's Tatak- idea) — powerful, one fixed form only.
30. **Vocal/unison stingers, theme-integrated SFX, sub-branded beds** — one per broadcast maximum.
31. **Figurative leads** ("Himas-rehas" style prison idiom) — one per broadcast, crime stories only.
32. **Bisaya/local-color greeting line** — free if a cast member speaks it; skip if not (C10/C16-style clean Filipino works).
33. **Anchor-read soft segments** (when a dedicated reporter is missing) — acceptable, but keep tone markers firing.
34. **Time-check-as-return after the break** — cheap realism.
35. **Digital/social plugs** — only if the concept is digital; cap at functional mentions.
36. **Editorial/commentary segment** — C18 is the only precedent; high risk for a 5-minute window; use a short moral close instead.

# 22. Common Weaknesses Observed

Recurring negative patterns (documented so future agents do not reproduce them; all DIRECTLY OBSERVED as transcript annotations):

1. **Pervasive delivery garbling (16/18)** — the corpus's dominant failure. English proper nouns, long subordinate clauses, and names at speed are the failure cluster. The top-four ranked entries all have clean news bodies; the bottom-ranked are the most garbled.
2. **Program-name/callsign instability on air (8/18)** — "NSBC", "MSPC", "PC Patron", "TBC JV 21.26" — the brand itself mispronounced, including in signature moments.
3. **Anchor self-name slips at transitions (4/18)** — "…MJ!" by MJ, "Brian, salamat!" by Brian; name mismatches between intro and sign-off (5/18).
4. **Missing or broken reporter sign-offs (5/18)** — breaking the corpus's own near-universal convention uncredits a story.
5. **Broken or delayed tease promises (4/18)** — the teaser promises films, the break delivers a PSA, no winner is ever named. The tease-reveal contract is the retention hinge; breaking it wastes the format's best device.
6. **Music-only dead gaps (~6/18)** — beds without dialogue (C2 ~13 s, C12 ~50 s, C13 17 s, C16 26 s) read as dead air.
7. **Weak or absent infomercial CTA (9/18)** — mood pieces without a "do X" instruction.
8. **Sourcing gaps (8/41 reports unattributed)** — including one unhedged, unattributed accusation (C5's "sabotahe").
9. **Factual contradictions on shared facts** — suspect age 20/29/binatilyo, $3.98/gal vs $70/gal, fire vs flood, PSC chairman name, "NBA Hall of Famer" Michael B. Jordan (C15) — all traceable to absent fact verification.
10. **Overlong openings (C5, C13, C16 at 81–88 s)** — survivable only with charming material.
11. **Dense SFX + dialogue failure (C2, C3 at 3.6/min)** — sound clutter competes with speech.
12. **Structural omissions are NOT the weakness** — missing sports (4/18) or showbiz (1/18) cost little; garbled *existing* content costs everything.

# 23. Division Competition Application

How we realistically use these National findings at Division level (ANALYST INFERENCE, framed from the corpus evidence):

1. **Achievable production:** the corpus's own best entries prove that 4–5 category beds, ~8–10 stingers, one time-check beep, and one diegetic cue are enough — C15 delivered a top-branded broadcast with 5 SFX events total, C10 and C16 with ~8–9. 3. **Concept clarity:** a simplified concept system (one slogan on a schedule + one sonic signature + a closing that answers the opening) is achievable; a full C5-style load-bearing metaphor is not required. The rule that matters: *introduce it early, pay it off at least twice.*

Choose a register (conversational partners OR formal presenters) and commit fully — both ends reached the top of the corpus.
6. **Memorability:** one invented slogan + one invented sound + one persona in the showbiz slot + a unison close. All free. All must be *fixed in form* before rehearsal (C17's drift is the cautionary tale).
7. **What NOT to attempt at Division level:** game-show infomercials are becoming a shared template — differentiate; comedy must stay in the licensed zones (showbiz + infomercial); no child-shaming; verify every statistic twice; rehearse names — especially the station's own.

# 25. AI AGENT IMPLEMENTATION RULES

Operational rules for any future AI agent asked to revise, rewrite, or extend our script.

## Before Revising a Script

Read, in order:
1. **Our current script** (the latest draft of our Division script);
2. **Our approved concept reference** (station identity, slogan, personas, concept decisions — the file where our team's chosen identity is locked);
3. **This benchmark** (NATIONAL-RADIO-SCRIPT-BENCHMARK.md) — and, when depth is needed, the underlying `national-script-analysis/` reports (per-script analyses for exemplars, specialist reports for the dimension being revised, cross-comparison reports for patterns and reusable techniques).

## Preserve

- **Factual content** — do not change facts, numbers, dates, names, or the source attribution of existing stories; if a fact conflicts with another draft, flag it, do not silently pick one.
- **Assigned roles** — do not swap which anchor/reporter owns which segment; do not add or remove cast without approval.
- **Approved branding** — the exact station name, callsign, frequency, program title, slogan, and sign-off formulas, in their exact approved forms.
- **Approved concept** — the chosen metaphor/vocabulary/sonic signature; maintain its fixed form (no improvisation, no variant drift).

## Apply

- **National-level pacing principles** (Section 18): first news ~60 s, 3-sentence reports, 25–35 s reporter blocks, advocacy ~45 s at ~2:35, soft block ~35 s each, close ~19 s; check every revision against the stopwatch profile.
- **Strong handoffs** (Section 9): name-question toss + thank-you receipt per reporter; at most one mid-report Q&A; proofread names in both lines.
- **Disciplined headlines** (Section 7): 2–4 items, `[TOPIC], [RESULT STATE]!`, 8–14 words, reveal hard/tease soft, one stinger per item, frame + handoff lines.
- **Sound-design logic** (Section 12): boundary-only stingers, beds ducking under speech, one function per sound, self-describing cue labels, crescendo-and-fade close.
- **Concise reporter writing** (Section 8): action-first lead, second-breath attribution, one statute, public advisory before the sign-off, named sign-off every report.
- **The infomercial skeleton** (Section 13): hook → scene → problem → fact → named solution → named CTA → slogan → return stinger, 40–50 s.
- **The closing ritual** (Section 16): recap → anchor IDs → station identity → music fade; include a theme callback to the opening.

## Do Not

- **Copy National competitor lines** — anything in Section 21 is off-limits, including "safe-sounding" generic lines that are actually competitor signatures.
- **Transplant their concept** — C5's travel system, C8's digital frame, C17's prefix brand, and the rest are property; our concept comes from our approved concept reference.
- **Overfill the script with SFX** — stay ≤ ~2 SFX/min, boundaries only; the corpus's failures (C2, C3) prove density competes with dialogue.
- **Make every line metaphorical** — concepts belong in the frame (open/transitions/close), not in the news body; the corpus keeps news prose plain.
- **Sacrifice clarity for creativity** — no forced rhyme inversions, no fragments posing as headlines, no unexplained chant; if a line would garble, rewrite it.
- **Fabricate facts** — every statistic in the infomercial and every number in news must trace to the fact sheet; hedge allegations with "umano/diumano".
- **Change the approved identity without approval** — consistency is the brand; the corpus's brand-drift entries all underperform.

## When Revising a Specific Section

- **Opening:** reuse the approved three-layer ID build; keep branding within the first 20 s; ensure the concept is revealed in the first 45 s and paid off later.
- **Headlines:** rewrite using the Section 7 formulas; never reveal soft-news outcomes; never exceed 14 words.
- **Transitions:** keep one connector per seam + bed change; ensure tease-then-promise at the break and identity on the return.
- **Infomercial:** apply Section 13 skeleton; verify the statistic; write a verb+object CTA naming an entity; keep the tone dignifying (no shaming frames).
- **Closing:** recap → IDs → station identity → theme callback → fade; ~19 s.
- **Delivery check:** read every line aloud in your head; flag any line with ≥2 unfamiliar English tokens, a subordinate-clause stack, or a name; gloss, shorten, or restructure it.

## When Reviewing a Draft

- Run the Section 26 checklist; score every dimension 1–5.
- Specifically check: name consistency everywhere (anchors, reporters, station, program); tease promises honored; no sign-off missing; no music-only gap > 5 s; total runtime within window; every fact traceable.

# 28. Final Synthesis

## THE NATIONAL-LEVEL PATTERN IN ONE SENTENCE

> A National-level script is a ~5-minute, two-act news broadcast — branded open, stingered headlines, 2–3 compressed and attributed hard-news reports, one dramatized infomercial, a post-break soft block, and a unison close — in which the same architecture is executed with disciplined timing, category-coded sound, scripted anchor chemistry, and — above all — lines that are written to be spoken and actually spoken cleanly.

## THE FIVE MOST IMPORTANT LESSONS

1. **Execution beats invention.** The corpus's widest quality gap is delivery cleanliness and brand-name stability, not structure or creativity. Clean lines + consistent names outperform clever lines + slips — every time.
2. **The architecture is expected; the execution differentiates.** Follow the 7-position spine and the pacing profile (first news ~60 s, advocacy ~45 s mid-show, close ~19 s), then compete on lead craft, handoffs, chemistry, and concept payoff.
3. **Sound marks structure, not sentences.** Category beds carry the genres, stingers mark boundaries only, beds duck under speech, and the infomercial absorbs the theatrical SFX. Restraint (C10, C15, C16) is a proven National-level option.
4. **Script the spontaneity.** Question tosses, thank-you receipts, split lines, reaction tokens, and unison money lines are all *written* — chemistry is manufactured on the page and delivered by rehearsal.
5. **A concept is a system, not a line.** Introduce it early, pay it off at least twice, give it one sound, and make the closing answer the opening — or don't carry it at all. A one-off slogan is a broken promise.

## THE BIGGEST MISTAKE TO AVOID

> Writing a script we cannot speak. The corpus's bottom-ranked entries all fail at the same point: lines that are structurally correct but orally impossible — stacked subordinate clauses, raw English proper nouns at speed, forced rhyme inversions, names at 3-second intervals — delivered without rehearsal, including the station's own name. The single most damaging, most avoidable mistake in the entire corpus is **an unrehearsed, unpronounceable script** — no amount of concept, sound, or structure survives a garbled delivery of the brand and the news. (CROSS-SCRIPT PATTERN: 16/18 entries, DIRECTLY OBSERVED)

## THE STANDARD OUR DIVISION SCRIPT SHOULD AIM FOR

Our Division script should be:
- **Structurally the equal of the National entries** — the same spine, the same timing discipline, the same segment balance;
- **Sonically identifiable but sparse** — a small bed map, boundary stingers, one logo, one diegetic cue;
- **Conceptually ours and cohesive** — one approved idea, paid off from open to close, in a fixed form;
- **Written for the voice** — grammatical, short, glossed, rehearsable, with a fact sheet behind every number;
- **Delivered cleanly** — drilled until the station name, the anchor names, the headlines, and the sign-offs are flawless, because that is the single trait the National corpus shows separates the top from the bottom.
