# Cross-Comparison 2 — Differences Between Teams: Where National-Level Scripts Legitimately Diverge

**Corpus:** 18 NSPC National-level radio broadcasting entries (`transcripts/Contestant-1 … Contestant-18/actual-script.md`). **Basis:** all 18 per-script analyses (`per-script/script-01-analysis.md` … `script-18-analysis.md`), all 13 specialist reports (`specialists/*.md`), and spot-checks of source transcripts where analyses conflicted. Mic-check phases excluded per corpus rules.

**Evidence labels:** **DIRECTLY OBSERVED** (quoted from a script/transcript) · **CROSS-SCRIPT PATTERN** (seen in multiple named entries) · **ANALYST INFERENCE** (interpretation, reasoning given). Entry shorthand: C1–C18. Where transcript corrections exist (`*[intended]*`, `[possibly: …]`), the intended reading is bracketed.

**Purpose:** Identify the areas where *successful* National-level scripts genuinely differ, so that no one reads this benchmark as prescribing a single correct formula. Each dimension below gives the observed spectrum, places entries on it with evidence, and answers the key question: **can both ends still be competitive?**

---

## 0. The Headline Finding

**CROSS-SCRIPT PATTERN (derived from the 18 analyses):** The corpus is highly uniform in *architecture* (see §15) and highly varied in *execution choices*. The specialist quality ranking (Specialist M) places at the top four entries that are near-opposites on every surface dimension: C16 (conversational warmth, sparse sound, no sports, concept confined to the close, slowest opening) ranks #1; C10 (uniformly formal register, lightest slip load, minimal branding, no concept system) ranks #2; C1 (dense anchor chemistry, balanced segments, game-show PSA) ranks #3; C5 (the corpus's only complete metaphor system, theme-driven sound) ranks #4. **(ANALYST INFERENCE)** The implication: **no surface dimension is decisive by itself.** The variables that separate the top from the bottom are execution-level — delivery cleanliness, brand-name stability, tease-reveal discipline — not whether the team is formal or playful, concept-heavy or plain, SFX-rich or sparse.

## 1. Anchor Register: Conversational "Partners" vs Formal "Presenters"

**Spectrum observed (DIRECTLY OBSERVED across entries):**

- **Fully conversational end:** C6 is the extreme — anchors speak in buddy register even inside news ("Naku [Ako], problema 'to, tandem.", "'Yun oh, eto tandem:", "Pero bago 'yan, masamang balita po."); C1's duet completion and banter ("MJ, ano bang nakahain ngayong araw?", the six-turn split scene chant); C13's nickname personas and pun bridge ("Mapapa-aray po tayo rito, Lucky Charm." / "Aba, bakit naman, Kuya Will?"); C4's closing banter that callbacks the top story ("Mahal na ng gasolina, partner."); C16's weather banter that seeds a news metaphor ("Tunay ngang mag-iinit dito sa City of Beautiful People, Partner Louie!" / "Aba d'yan, Lian!").
- **Fully formal end:** C10 is the extreme — deliberately literary everywhere, even showbiz ("Sa gitna ng mainit na labanan, lumamig sa halu-halong emosyon ang mga manonood…") and zero banter; C9's "well-drilled announcers" (per-script analysis 9); C3's procedural co-anchors with only one warm moment (the split time check); C2, C11, C12's "readers" tier with functional handoffs and no interpersonal exchange (Specialist C tiers).
- **Middle ("hosts with a sidekick"):** C8, C14, C15, C17 — agreement exchanges ("Tama ka dyan, Jela."), question handoffs ("Zal Ramos, ano ang latest?"), one anchor leads most turns.

**Can both ends compete? YES.** Specialist M ranks C10 (the most formal entry) #2 and C16 (a "Partners"-tier conversational duo) #1. The formal end works because C10's register is backed by the lightest delivery-error load in the corpus (~4–5 corrections) — the polish justifies the stiffness (Specialist M, A2). The conversational end works because the chemistry is *designed and rehearsed*, not improvised (Specialist C: "Partners" tier entries use split sentences, Q&A banter, and unison lines). **The danger zone is the middle-lower tiers — the "Readers" (C2, C3, C7, C9, C11, C12) — where handoffs are clean but zero interpersonal layer exists; those pairs are "competent, interchangeable" (Specialist C).** Verdict: choose *either* a fully realized partner register *or* a fully realized formal register; the losing configuration is a formal script with no polish, or a casual register that flattens the news/soft-news contrast (Specialist J flags C6's consistent casualness as the risk of its personality choice).

## 2. Central Concept: Complete System vs Subtle / No Concept

**Spectrum observed (Specialist L's classification, DIRECTLY OBSERVED evidence):**

- **Complete systems (2/18):** C5's travel/road-trip metaphor — concept vocabulary at open ("handa ka na ba sa biyahe natin ngayon?"), headlines ("Una sa ating ruta…"), break ("red light muna"), and close ("Nakarating na ang ating biyahe sa araw na ito") with a sonic signature (car horn "Pipip!"); C8's digital-native identity — "Tama muna pag-scroll" open, app/FYP/hashtag plugs as functional handoffs, unison **"Offline!"** close. Both satisfy the four-part test: vocabulary at open/mid/close, closing answers opening, sound belongs to the concept, break folded into the concept (Specialist L §2).
- **Partial systems (many):** C1 (meal/coffee frame + split slogan "Pilipinas… balitaan na!"), C3 (truth bookend "katotohanan ang ating sandigan"), C4 (open-loaded "walang pinapanigan" ×3, never returns), C6 (tandem vocabulary ~7×, no sonic echo), C9 ("Kasama Ka" bookends, hollow middle), C10 (reporter sign-off formula as the only system), C13 (four stacked micro-brands: chant, jingle, nicknames, "Patrol ng Bayan"), C14 and C16 (close-loaded brands — taglines/creed held until the final 15–25 s), C17 (the "Tatak-" prefix system, best idea, imperfect execution — variants drift totoo→tutok→patrol), C18 (personal catchphrase bookended, no program frame).
- **No concept / micro-brands only:** C2, C12 (nothing recoverable), C11, C15 (richest *collection* of micro-brands — anthem, tri-region call, "Special Updates" stinger, Tindahan ni Nena, Mamshie Kirby, closing chant — with no unifying metaphor).

**Can both ends compete? YES, with a caveat.** The corpus's #1 and #2 entries are *not* complete-concept entries: C16's concept lives entirely in the closing creed, C10's is a sign-off formula. A complete system is not required to win — but Specialist L's rule holds across the corpus: a concept that appears once is a broken promise ("Ulam na!" C4, "Ang totoong boses ng Ormoc" C7, the closing chant "Ang kikisig! Husto! Aksyon!" C15 with no referent). **The competitive distinction is not "concept present vs absent" but "concept paid off at least twice vs concept abandoned."** The gimmick-risk ranking (Specialist L §7) places C5 lowest-risk and C15's close-chant highest; C17's near-miss shows that even the best prefix idea fails if variants slip.

## 3. Sound Design: Dense SFX vs Restrained SFX

**Spectrum observed (Specialist G counts of documented cue labels; DIRECTLY OBSERVED):**

- **Dense band (≥3.0 SFX/min):** C2 (17 SFX events, 3.6/min — stinger before every headline *and* mid-cues inside the anchor/reporter exchange), C3 (17 events, 3.6/min — four SFX in the ~30 s AI-infomercial scene: keyboard typing, robotic chime, thunder drop, evil laugh).
- **Moderate band (2.0–2.9/min):** C4, C5, C7, C8, C9, C14, C17.
- **Sparse band (≤1.9/min):** C1, C6, C10, C11, C12, C13, C16, C18; **C15 is the extreme at 1.0/min** — 5 SFX events in the whole broadcast (two headline beeps, "Pogi!" bumper, two Special-Updates stingers) — yet Specialist G calls it "among the most branded entries in the corpus."

**Can both ends compete? YES.** The #1 entry (C16) runs 1.5 SFX/min; the #2 entry (C10) runs 1.9/min with "the cleanest news-bed labeling"; C15's ultra-sparse design demonstrates restraint as a deliberate option. The two densest entries (C2, C3) sit in the bottom half — but Specialist G explicitly flags the correlation is not causal: "Dense stinger use does not rescue weak delivery; it competes with it." The functional rule that *does* hold across all 18 (CROSS-SCRIPT PATTERN): beds carry categories, stingers mark boundaries, beds duck under speech ("UP AND UNDER" in ≥16/18), and theatrical SFX are concentrated in the infomercial zone. **Both ends are competitive as long as the one-function-per-sound discipline holds; the failure mode is density *plus* dialogue failure (C2, C3), not density alone.** C14 (moderate, 2.0/min) shows the middle can win too, with the corpus's best self-describing cue labels (whistle for sports, sparkle for showbiz).

## 4. Comedy-Heavy vs Serious

**Spectrum observed (DIRECTLY OBSERVED):**

- **Comedy-heavy:** C1 (pahulaan guessing game, "Check na check!", "Tumpak!"), C10 (dark-humor PSA — the neighbor jokes "parang masarap din 'yang kainin!" about a pet dog), C13 (pun banter, "Heto na!" chant), C15 ("Pogi!" bumper, basketball/Oscar crossover puns in showbiz, "Bangon, may chika!"), C8 (persona comedy — "Mare, alam niyo ba…", "Yas Queen" team chant), C18 (DepEd quiz with comedic wrong answers: "Department of Entertainment and Drama!"), C12 (Chika-Dora / Grace Bumbina pun names), C16 (weather banter, "Aba d'yan, Lian!").
- **Serious throughout:** C7 (no showbiz at all; the broadcast goes sports → political editorial "Tayo'y tumindig sa tama at labanan ang pandarambong!" and never relaxes), C11 (showbiz as serious awards journalism with critic analysis — "ayon sa mga kritiko… kampanya sa promo"), C2 (zero humor anywhere), C3 (dignified civic thesis), C9 (no banter, no jokes).

**Where humor lives (CROSS-SCRIPT PATTERN, Specialist I):** humor is almost exclusively licensed in **two zones** — the showbiz segment (persona-based, not joke-based: "the joke is *who* is talking") and the infomercial drama. Sports stays earnest in every entry (the shared stranded-athlete story suppresses comedy). No entry attempts comedy inside hard news.

**Can both ends compete? YES.** C7 (fully serious, no showbiz) and C3 (serious thesis) are mid-tier (#12, #11); C10 (comedy PSA) is #2; C1 (game-show PSA) is #3; C18 (comedy quiz) is #9. The constraint is placement, not presence: teams that are funny in the *licensed zones* and serious in the news body rank highest; teams that are serious everywhere forfeit the tone-shift opportunity the soft half is built for (Specialist I: C7 "forgoes the genre contrast entirely"). **Taste boundaries are where comedy fails:** C10's dog-eating joke, C6's "Mahina si Ruben" child-shaming, and C13's mother-shaming ("Ang tanda mo na? Hindi ka pa rin magaling magbasa?") are the corpus's flagged risks (Specialist H §7); C18's self-deprecating wrong answers are the model of safe comedy.

## 5. Headlines: Many vs Few, Long vs Short

**Spectrum observed (Specialist D counts; DIRECTLY OBSERVED):**

- **Count:** 2 headlines — C1, C3, C7, C8, C9, C10, C15, C16, C17 (9/18); 3 headlines — C14, C18; 4 headlines — C4, C5, C11, C12, C13. (C2 has 4 slots, one fragmented into two parts — see §14 conflict note.)
- **Length:** C4 compresses to 6–8 words per headline; C10's second headline runs ~20 words ("Ang 10-araw na extension sa deadline ng pagpapabukas sa Strait of Hormuz ay pinataw ni US President Donald Trump laban sa Iran!"); C17's both run 18–20 words. The 8–14 word band is the mode.
- **Syntax:** the dominant shape is `[TOPIC], [RESULT STATE]!` — e.g., C16 "Iligal na pagbebenta ng financial accounts, nabisto ng pulisya!"; C18 "ATP Challenger 50 Fujairah Open, naudlot! Pilipinong tennis player Francis Alcantara, hindi natuloy!"
- **Tease-vs-reveal:** reveal-dominant (hard news states the outcome) vs tease-dominant (C5, C13 use question-form teases for sports/showbiz: "sino-sinong artist… ang wagi sa Oscars 2026?"). The corpus rule that emerges: **reveal the hard, tease the soft** (CROSS-SCRIPT PATTERN — no entry ever teases hard news with a question).

**Can both ends compete? YES — count is the weakest variable.** The #1 entry (C16) runs only 2 headlines; the #2 entry (C10) runs 2. The 4-headline entries include both the strongest rundowns (C4, C5 per Specialist D) and mid/bottom-tier entries (C12, C11). **What does discriminate:** (a) coverage — a 2-headline block that teases nothing about sports/showbiz (C15) wastes the rundown; (b) length — 18–20-word headlines (C17) exceed the breath window; (c) the frame+handoff glue ("Sa detalye ng mga balita!" C4, "Simulan na ang balitaan!" C17) that makes a staccato list feel like a program (Specialist D §6). Both a 2-item and a 4-item rundown can win; a 2-item rundown that covers only half the broadcast cannot fully use the format's retention hinge.

## 6. News Stories: Two vs Three

**Spectrum observed (Specialist A table; DIRECTLY OBSERVED):**

- **2 hard-news stories:** 13 entries (C1, C2, C3, C4, C5, C8, C9, C10, C11, C14, C15, C17, C18).
- **3 hard-news stories:** 5 entries (C6, C7, C12, C13, C16). Notably, **4 of the 5 three-story entries drop sports** (C6, C12, C13, C16) — the extra news story is the trade for the soft slot.
- Every entry has ≥2 and ≤3 hard-news reports; the news pool itself is shared (the Ormoc scam, WPS cyanide, Middle East oil, Alcantara, Oscars — 14–18 of 18 entries per story, Specialist E §1).

**Can both ends compete? YES.** C16 (3 stories, no sports) is Specialist M's #1; C1 (2 stories, full segment set) is #3; C13 (3 stories, no sports) is #7. The trade is real and both sides are represented in the top half. Specialist M explicitly notes structural completeness "matters less than expected": C16 omits sports yet ranks first; C12 includes the full template and ranks last. **The discriminator is never the count — it is whether the *chosen* stories are delivered cleanly and with attribution.** The 2-story entries that fail (C2, C9, C11) fail on delivery, not on count.

## 7. Sports & Showbiz: Present, Anchor-Read, or Absent

**Spectrum observed (Specialist I; DIRECTLY OBSERVED):**

- **Sports:** present with a dedicated sportscaster in 14/18; **absent in 4** (C6, C12, C13, C16); **anchor-read** in C10 (Ezra reads and signs his own sports report, "Ezra Luague, nagmamasid sa mga balitang napapanahon").
- **Showbiz:** present in 17/18; **absent in C7** (the only entry that never leaves its serious register); **anchor-read in C4** (16 s, the shortest showbiz in the corpus, no sign-off).
- **Order (when both exist, 13 entries):** sports→showbiz in 10 (C1, C2, C4, C8, C9, C10, C14, C15, C17, C18); showbiz→sports in 3 (C3, C5, C11). Specialist A finds "no structural penalty observable" for the inversion.
- **Category differentiation when present:** every entry gives each soft category its own music bed; strong entries add category-flavored sign-offs (C10's one-word swap "balitang napapanahon" → "chika na napapanahon" is the model; C17 carries "Tatak-totoo!" into both soft sign-offs).

**Can both ends compete? YES.** The #1 entry omits sports entirely; C7's omission of showbiz is coherent (an activist editorial close) though it costs the broadcast its only guaranteed tone-shift. What the absent-segment entries share is *substitution*, not loss: C6/C16 fill the slot with an international report, and showbiz carries the entire soft half. The losing configuration is not the omission — it is a soft segment present but *flat*: C4's 16-second anchor-read showbiz and C10's anchor-read sports are the corpus's weakest soft segments because the tone markers fire but the personality is absent (Specialist I §8).

## 8. Branding: Elaborate Multi-Layer vs Minimal

**Spectrum observed (Specialist B; DIRECTLY OBSERVED):**

- **Elaborate end:** C4's three-layer ID (patriotic jingle slogan → frequency sweep → "DCJV 91.26!" → drum hit → "NSPC Patrol!" — "the complete ID architecture," ranked #1 opening in the corpus); C13's jingle stanza → chorus → announcer chain; C7's built ID (drum roll → "Ito ang…" → accent → "DZJV-9126!" → "SPC Patrol!"); C15's sung theme — the only vocal theme song in the corpus; C1's bookended branding (slogan at open, callsign at close).
- **Minimal end:** C18 brands **nowhere in the opening** — the first full ID arrives at the 07:58 sign-off ("Direk sa DCJV 91.26 NSPC Patrol!"); C11 has no callsign anywhere and its only ID is garbled ("Ito ang MSPC [NSPC]… PC Patron [Patrol]!" at ~50 s); C10 speaks its program name exactly twice and has no callsign/frequency at all; C17 has no callsign (name + "Tatak-totoo!" only); C3 has no callsign or frequency anywhere.
- **Latency:** 0–3 s branding (C1, C4, C5, C6, C8, C10, C12, C15, C16) vs 28–52 s (C2, C9, C11, C13, C15's explicit ID) vs never-in-opening (C18).

**Can both ends compete? PARTIALLY — this is the one dimension with a hard boundary.** Specialist B's principle #1: every opening with branding latency ≥28 s (C2, C9, C11, C13, C15, C18) is among the weakest; every top-ranked opening brands within 20 s. **But "minimal repetition" is fine — the boundary is about *when the first ID lands*, not *how often it repeats*.** C10 (exactly two name mentions in 5 minutes) ranks #2; C17 (no callsign, slogan system) ranks #6. The losing configuration is C18's personality-first opening that defers all identity to the sign-off — "a listener joining mid-open cannot know the station or program" (Specialist B). Also non-negotiable: **name stability** — every top-half entry says "NSPC Patrol" correctly every time; entries with brand drift (C2's NSBC, C9's NSBC, C11's MSPC/LSPC, C12's "NSP, si Patron", C15's "NSBC!") all sit in the bottom half (Specialist M's brand-stability finding).

## 9. Opening Architecture & Length

**Spectrum observed (Specialists A, B, K; DIRECTLY OBSERVED):**

- **Architecture:** ID-first (12/18: C1, C4, C5, C6, C7, C10, C12, C14, C15, C16, C17, C18) vs headlines-first (4/18: C2, C8, C11, C13) vs blended (C3, C9). Two additional variants: time-check-first (C16, C18 — the first sound is the clock) and pure-music-runways (C11's 17 s of music before any voice; C12's ~20 s gap after the ID).
- **Length to first news:** 52 s (C17) to 88 s (C16); median ≈ 63 s; ~17–29% of the broadcast. The two extremes are both top-tier entries.
- **Content of the open:** all 18 include theme/OBB, station ID, greeting (often with a Bisaya line), date check (14/18), anchor self-intro, and stingered headlines; the ordering is what differs.

**Can both ends compete? YES.** C16's 88 s open (the corpus's slowest) is the #1 entry — Specialist M: "the riskiest open in the corpus, mitigated only by the quality of the material"; C17's 52 s (fastest) is #6. Headlines-first opens exist at both ends (C13's "Heto na!" hook is #5-ranked opening; C2's is #2-worst) — the difference is whether the hook *earns* the delayed branding (C13's chant does; C2's garbled headlines don't). **The rule is not length but fill:** the open must be packed with identity beats, not dead air — C4's 71 s are "filled with identity beats" while C12's two good lines are separated by a ~20 s music gap (Specialist B).

## 10. Closing: Minimal Gesture vs Elaborate Ritual

**Spectrum observed (Specialist A; DIRECTLY OBSERVED):**

- **Minimal end:** C8 (13 s — one social plug + unison "Offline!"), C13 (12 s — recap line + "LABAN, PILIPINAS!").
- **Elaborate end:** C17 (33 s — moral spiel "Tatak-patrol, may malasakit at paninindigan…" + call-and-response ID + Bisaya farewell + "Magandang araw, Pilipinas!"), C16 (25 s — the seven-line parallel creed "hindi sapat ang makinig… maging mapanuri!" — the corpus's best-structured close), C18 (25 s — recap, Bisaya self-ID, TD credit, ensemble ID + national-pride slogan), C6 (two-voice alternating blessing ending in unison "Ormoc, Ormoc, magpadayon pa!").
- **Closing anatomy (18/18):** recap line → anchor IDs → station identity/slogan → outro swell-fade. Extensions beyond the median: moral spiels (C3, C5, C6, C10, C13, C16, C17), editorial (C18), banter/reminder (C4), digital plug (C8).

**Can both ends compete? YES.** C13's 12 s close and C8's 13 s close sit in the top half of Specialist M's ranking (#7, #10); C17's 33 s close is #6; C16's 25 s creed close is #1. Specialist L's close-loaded entries (C14, C16) show a closing-only brand "functions as a system only if the listener stays to the end — which, for a 5-minute judged broadcast, is nearly everyone." **The competitive constant is completion mechanics** (recap or gesture + identity + music fade — present 18/18), not duration. The failure modes are specific: a recap that names no story (C1's generic line), a garbled final ID (C15's "ABCJP 9126, NSBC!"), or an emotionally strong close that abandons brand duty (C3, C6 — no station name in the final 90 s).

## 11. Reporter Sign-Off Systems: Uniform vs Category-Varied vs Broken

**Spectrum observed (Specialists E, I; DIRECTLY OBSERVED):**

- **Uniform single formula:** C6 — every reporter signs "Nagpapatrol, [Name]" (news and showbiz alike); C10 — "nagmamasid sa mga balitang napapanahon" with the one-word showbiz swap "chika".
- **Category-varied family:** C1 (hard news = program identity "ulat sa NSPC Patrol"; sports = "inyong Sports Patroller!"; showbiz = "nagpapatrol sa showbiz chika!"); C15 (the b-verb family: "bumabalita para sa bayan/masa" → "bumabangon para sa balitang sports" → "Bangon, may chika!"); C18 (four distinct formulas: "NSPC Patroller" / "Nakatutok sa balita" / "sports authority" / "for the showbiz update"); C17 ("Para sa NSPC Patrol, [Name], Tatak-totoo!" — slogan fused into every sign-off).
- **Broken/inconsistent:** C7 (the local reporter's sign-off has no name — "Live mula sa Ormoc City!"); C16 (Angel's national report has no sign-off at all, breaking the entry's own rule); C11 (local report ends without a sign-off); C8 (Jazz gets none while John gets a tagline).

**Can both ends compete? YES — uniformity and variety both work.** The uniform systems (C6, C10) are simple, predictable, and brand-reinforcing; the varied families (C1, C15, C18) use the sign-off itself as a genre signal. Specialist E's verdict: the two strongest families are beat-persona sign-offs ("Nakatutok sa balita…") and station-tagline fusion ("Tatak-totoo"). **The failure mode is inconsistency-within-one-broadcast** (some reporters sign off, others don't — C16 breaks its own pattern exactly once, C8's Jazz is missing) and sign-offs delivered garbled (C13's all three, C14's two of three, C5's "Showbiz but shoulder, can't believe it" for "Showbiz Patroller, Kenneth!"). Sign-off lines are "the most rehearsable lines in radio" (Specialist M) — their failure is a preparation signal, not a design choice.

## 12. Anchor-to-Reporter Interaction: Live Q&A vs Straight Monologue

**Spectrum observed (Specialists C, E; DIRECTLY OBSERVED):**

- **Q&A used (8/18):** C1, C2, C4, C7, C8, C11, C13, C14 — the anchor re-enters mid-report with a follow-up question ("Ah, Janine, may nahuli na nga ba?" C1; "Nasaan na ngayon ang sospek?" C13; "Dito sa Pilipinas, anong aksyon ang ginawa ng ating gobyerno?" C4; "Jazz, ano ba ang epekto nito sa mga mangingisda roon?" C8). Also question-format handoffs in 12/18 ("Zal Ramos, ano ang latest?" C17; "J, what's the scoop?" C3; "Mamshie Kirby, anong chika?" C15).
- **Monologue-only (10/18):** reports run as single uninterrupted blocks with a sign-off and thank-you, no anchor interjection (C3, C5, C6, C9, C10, C12, C15, C16, C17, C18 — though C6, C9, C17 use question *handoffs*).

**Can both ends compete? YES.** Four of the top-five entries (C1, C13, C14, C16 — C10 being the exception) use at least one mid-report Q&A, and Specialist C calls it "the format's strongest realism device." But monologue-only entries (C16's world report + Q&A is the model; C17's clean three-voice co-read) rank at the top too — C16 *is* the #1 entry and uses exactly one Q&A. **The Q&A is an amplifier, not a requirement**; what matters is that the pre-answerable question ("what happens next / what can be filed?") is scripted and rehearsed — C7's botched mid-report question (the anchor asks himself, "Prince, ano namang aksyon sa suspect?") shows how easily the device backfires (Specialist C §10.1). The same pattern holds for handoff formulas: "Narito si X", "Nakatutok si X", and name-question forms all appear across the top half.

## 13. Infomercial: Format, Duration, and Fact-Discipline

**Spectrum observed (Specialist H; DIRECTLY OBSERVED):**

- **Format:** dramatized skit (12/18 — classroom dramas, mother-child scenes, C15's "Tindahan ni Nena", C5's in-car traffic scene) vs game-show/quiz (C1's Pahulaan, C18's DepEd quiz) vs chant/call-and-response (C7, C17) vs unintelligible (C12) vs untranscribed (C8, 65 s placeholder).
- **Duration:** 35 s (C2) to 69 s (C8); mean ≈ 48 s; the median band 40–50 s. In most broadcasts the infomercial is the single longest segment.
- **Fact discipline:** statistic+source+CTA chains (C13's ARAL: "Ayon sa pag-aaral, 30 to 40 percent… nag-improve dahil sa ARAL Program" → "sama-sama nating suportahan ang DepEd ARAL Program"; C7's "I-report ang mga paglabag sa Animal Welfare Act!") vs no-statistic, no-CTA mood pieces (C3, C6, C9, C14, C16, C17).

**Can both ends compete? YES.** Game-show formats appear in two top-half entries (C1 #3, C18 #9); skits dominate everywhere; C16's testimonial PSA (three named learners + ensemble slogan) belongs to the #1 entry; C10's confrontational skit belongs to the #2 entry. **The duration extremes both fail** — C7/C8's 64–69 s PSAs crowd the news (Specialist H: "the ceiling where a mid-broadcast PSA starts crowding the news"), and C2's 35 s is the shortest *and* one of the most garbled. **The true discriminator is the CTA and the statistic** — 9/18 entries have weak or absent calls to action, and mangled numbers (C4's "1%" inversion, C7's missing numerator) are the corpus's most damaging content-level errors. The integration rule holds across all formats: promised before the break (17/18) and re-anchored after (13/18) — C12, the only entry with no break promise, is also the bottom-ranked.

## 14. Conflict Resolutions (Spot-Checks Against Sources)

Where analyses disagreed, the source transcripts were checked. Results:

1. **C5's post-break "station tag" — RESOLVED.** Per-script analysis 5's transition table labeled "PSA → Program | Bumper accent + station tag | 06:22 | Clean," which Specialists A and F read as *no program re-entry* ("C5 goes straight into anchor banter"). **Spot-check of `transcripts/Contestant-5/actual-script.md` (06:22–06:25):** the line at 06:22–06:24 is `**PSA NARRATOR / STATION TAG:** Ang susampalang *[paalalang]* ito ay aking *[hatid]* sa inyo na may pilang *[ng himpilang]* ito` — the standard PSA sponsorship tag ("this reminder is brought to you by this station"), spoken by the PSA narrator, **not** a program re-entry ID. ## 16. Synthesis: What "No Single Formula" Actually Means

**(ANALYST INFERENCE, built on the evidence above)**

1. **The differences that matter are execution differences, not surface choices.** Formal (C10) and conversational (C1, C6, C16) both reach the top; concept-complete (C5) and concept-minimal (C10, C16) both reach the top; 2 headlines (C16, C10) and 4 headlines (C5) both reach the top; no-sports (C16) and full-segment-set (C1) both reach the top; 88 s opens (C16) and 52 s opens (C17) both reach the top; 12 s closes (C13) and 33 s closes (C17) both reach the top.
2. **The hard boundaries — the few places one end is provably wrong:** (a) branding must land in the first ~20 s (every ≥28 s latency open is weak); (b) the brand name must be pronounced identically every time (top half vs bottom half correlate perfectly with name stability); (c) the news body must be delivered in grammatical, recoverable sentences (C10/C16 vs C2/C12 is "the widest quality gap in the entire benchmark" — Specialist M); (d) teases must be honored (C16's films tease never answered; C7's headline 1 never reported; C14's "who won?" never named); (e) formulas must be consistent once adopted (sign-offs, slogan variants — C17's Tatak- drift and C8's missing Jazz sign-off are the cautionary cases).
3. **The genuinely flexible choices** — where a Division-level team should feel free to pick either end: anchor register (pick one and commit), concept depth (complete system, bookended system, or none — but never a one-off), SFX density (sparse through moderate is proven safe; dense requires flawless dialogue), comedy placement (licensed zones only), headline count (2–4, but cover the segments you run and keep items ≤14 words), story count (2–3, with the sports-trade explicitly viable), soft-segment set (sports optional; showbiz nearly universal; if you drop one, substitute news or a longer close), sign-off system (uniform or varied, but complete), close length (12–33 s, keep the four completion beats).
4. **The one choice with no proven winning precedent:** C18's personality-first opening with zero branding (never in the opening) is the only configuration the corpus consistently flags as a branding failure — avoid it even though the entry's anchor work is fine (Specialist B).
