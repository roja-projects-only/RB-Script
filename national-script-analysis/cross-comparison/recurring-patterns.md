## 1. Structural / Architectural Patterns

### P1. The two-act "Patrol" broadcast skeleton
**Count:** 18/18. **Classification: NEAR-UNIVERSAL.**
Every entry runs the same seven-position spine: **branded open → 2–3 hard-news reports → break tease → exactly one infomercial → re-entry → soft block (sports/showbiz) → closing** (Specialist A, §3.1, §9.3).
- C1: open (02:49–03:51) → local → national → teaser → infomercial → sports → showbiz → close.
- C18: open → national → local → teaser → DepEd infomercial → sports → showbiz → editorial → close (11 segments, the largest).
**Purpose (ANALYST INFERENCE):** the infomercial is a mandatory "demonstrate the advocacy genre" slot; placing it mid-broadcast separates the serious news half from the lighter soft half, giving the ~5-minute format two tonal acts. Strength of evidence: HIGH — derived from all 18 rundown tables (DIRECTLY OBSERVED).

### P2. "…Patrol" program frame with invented station identity
**Count:** 18/18 use "NSPC Patrol" as the metadata program title (C7 also says "SPC Patrol" on air; C8 is "NSPC Patrol Online"). **14/18** add an invented callsign + frequency; **~13/18** reuse the "91.26" pattern (DZJV, DZJB, DCJV, DZJP, DCJT, PCTV, PCJV…). C3, C10, C11, C17 brand by name only. **Classification: NEAR-UNIVERSAL (the frame); the individual callsigns are TEAM-SPECIFIC identities.**
- C4: "DCJV 91.26! … NSPC Patrol!" (03:19–03:29).
- C13: "Ito ang SPC Patrol sa DZJP 91.26."
**Purpose:** the "patrol" verb family is then recycled into reporter roles ("Patroller", "Sports Patroller", "nagpapatrol, [name]", "NSPC Patroller") and audience coinages ("ka-patrol", "mga kapatrol") — a full identity system. Strength: HIGH (corpus-inventory §4; all 18 metadata tables).

### P3. Branded open before the first news report
**Count:** 18/18. First full news report begins at 52–88 s (mean ≈ 66 s, median ≈ 64 s) after broadcast start; the open block (OBB/ID/greeting/headlines) consumes ≈17–29% of airtime. **Classification: NEAR-UNIVERSAL.**
- C16 (slowest): time check → 26 s music → ID → greeting → date → joint ID → banter → headlines; first story at 88 s.
- C17 (fastest): first story at ~52 s.
**Purpose:** branding is treated as part of the judged performance, not overhead (Specialist A §7; Specialist K §3.1). Strength: HIGH (timestamps in all 18 rundowns).

### P4. Closing ritual: recap line → anchor self-ID → station identity/slogan → music fade
**Count:** 18/18. Closing length 12–33 s (median ≈ 19 s). **Classification: NEAR-UNIVERSAL.**
- C1: "At iyan po ang mga balitang inyong tinutukan ngayong araw!" → "Parehong oras pa rin tayo bukas ha…" → chorus "DZJV 91.26 NSPC Patrol!" → "Mabuhay ka!" → fade.
- C18: recap → Bisaya self-ID → TD credit → ensemble "Direk sa DCJV 91.26 NSPC Patrol! Mabuhay ang magiting na radyo peryodistang Pilipino!" → fade.
**Purpose:** the close "completes the loop" — the same identity that opened the broadcast ends it (station ID at both ends in all 18 entries, Specialist A §9.2). Strength: HIGH.

### P5. Unison / ensemble "money line" at the ID or sign-off
**Count:** 18/18 have at least one line delivered by both anchors or the full ensemble in unison; unison lines cluster at open and close, never mid-broadcast. **Classification: NEAR-UNIVERSAL.**
- C4: "Oras na sa mga balita ang walang pinapanika [pinapanigan]!" (unison, 03:59).
- C7: "Sa muli't muli, ito ang NSPC Patrol!" (07:49); C8: "Offline!"; C13: "LABAN, PILIPINAS!".
**Purpose:** synchronized delivery is the cheapest chemistry proof and converts the duo into a single voice exactly when the station identity must land (Specialist C §2.2). Strength: HIGH.

### P6. The shared news pool (same five stories, one "news day")
**Count:** the Ormoc ATM-scam arrest appears in 14/18 (all but C3, C5, C10, C17), the WPS cyanide story in 15/18 (all but C4, C14, C15), the Middle-East oil story in 12/18, the Alcantara/Fujairah story in 14/18, the Oscars-2026 story in 17/18. **Classification: NEAR-UNIVERSAL (as a corpus-level fact).**
- C1: "Timbog sa ikinasang operasyon ng Ormoc City Cyber Response Team ang isang scammer…" vs C18: "Matagumpay na nahuli ng Ormoc City Cyber Response Team ang 29-anyos na lalaki…" — same event, different writing.
**Purpose:** teams are scored on *how* they write the same facts — lead craft, attribution, compression, sign-offs (Specialist E §1). The same fact pack also produces cross-entry contradictions (suspect age 20/29/binatilyo; oil $3.98/gal vs $70/gal; fire vs flood) — evidence of an imperfect shared source sheet. Strength: HIGH (41 reports inventoried).

## 2. Opening & Branding Patterns

### P7. Line-one or first-20-seconds station ID (three-layer ID build)
**Count:** 10/18 brand within the first 3 seconds (C1, C3 attempt, C4, C5, C6, C8, C10, C12, C15, C16); 3/18 within 13–20 s (C7, C14, C17); 5/18 at 28–52 s (C2, C9, C11, C13, C15-explicit); C18 never brands in the opening. The strongest IDs are built as a three-layer sequence **promise/slogan → callsign+frequency → program name** (C4, C13, C14, C16). **Classification: NEAR-UNIVERSAL (an ID in the first ~20 s); the three-layer build is OPTIONAL (4/18).**
- C4: jingle slogan → `[FREQUENCY SWEEP]` "DCJV 91.26!" → "Sa tapat at maasahang balita, walang pinapanika [pinapanigan]!" → `[DRUM HIT]` "NSPC Patrol!".
- C18 (failure case): no ID at all until the 07:58 sign-off.
**Purpose:** the listener's "what station is this?" must be answered before the second story question arises; every opening with branding latency ≥28 s is also a weak opening (Specialist B §3.2, §11). Strength: HIGH.

### P8. Time check (with or without beep) as a realism cue
**Count:** 12/18 formal time checks (C1, C2, C3, C4, C6, C9, C11, C13, C14, C15, C16, C18; see Resolution 3); 8/18 pair it with a beep/chime (`[SFX: TIME CHECK BEEP]` C1, C2, C3, C4, C6; `[TIME CHECK CHIME]` C11, C14; `[TIME CHECK BUMPER]` C9). **Classification: COMMON.**
- C1: "Oras ngayon na alauna ng hapon." + `[SFX: TIME CHECK BEEP]`.
- C16: "Oras nang Pilipinas: 4:06 ng hapon. At magsisimula na ang NSPC Patrol!" (time check as the broadcast's first sound).
**Purpose:** clock realism is the cheapest authenticity device — it reproduces the real Radyo Patrol "Oras sa Pilipinas" ritual and doubles as an attention reset (Specialist G §8.1). Strength: HIGH.

### P9. Role-titled anchor self-introduction ("Ako ang inyong [role], [name]")
**Count:** 7/18 (C4, C5, C7, C10, C16, C17, C18); the remaining entries use bare "Ako si X" (C9, C11) or no names at all (C3). **Classification: COMMON.**
- C5: "Ako ang inyong travel buddy sa Balita, Ralph Corton."
- C17: "Ito ang inyong tagapagbalita, Ivan Galupo."
- C18: "Ako ang inyong lingkod, Gia Ruzano."
**Purpose:** pairing the name with a service role makes the ID about the listener, not the talent — and every top-ranked opening uses it (Specialist B §7.1). Strength: HIGH.

### P10. A repeatable slogan/tagline used on a schedule (open → mid → close)
**Count:** 8/18 deploy a slogan that returns at least twice beyond the opening (C1 "Pilipinas… balitaan na!"; C4 "walang pinapanigan" ×3 in the first minute; C6 "tandem/katandem" ~10 uses; C8 "online/Offline!"; C9 "Kasama Ka" bookend; C13 "Heto na!" + "Patrol ng Bayan"; C17 "Tatak-totoo!"; C18 "Walang preno, tuloy-tuloy" bookend). **Classification: COMMON.**
- C17 (best system): "Tatak-totoo!" at 03:12, adapted as "Tatak-tutok!" (05:56), appended to three reporter sign-offs ("Para sa NSPC Patrol, Kian Sandido, Tatak-totoo!"), and expanded at close ("Tatak-patrol, may malasakit at paninindigan…").
**Purpose:** a slogan spoken once is decoration; the memory payoff comes from scheduled repetition — opening, mid-show (sign-offs/bumpers), and close (Specialist B §5.2, Specialist L §6). Strength: HIGH.

### P11. Bilingual local-color line (Bisaya/Cebuano greeting or farewell)
**Count:** 10/18 (C4, C8, C9, C10, C11, C13, C14, C16, C17, C18; C6 adds a Bisaya tagline). **Classification: COMMON.**
- C17: "Maayong adlaw, silong sa Ormoc!" (open) ↔ "Ug maayong adlaw sa inyong tanan!" (close).
- C18: "Maayong hapon, dakbayan sa Ormoc!"
**Purpose:** two lines of local language buy a "home station" identity and give the co-anchor a distinct vocal role (Specialist C §2.3; Specialist J §1). Strength: HIGH.

### P12. Central concept revealed in the first ~45 s and paid off later
**Count:** ~12/18 announce some concept in the open; only 2/18 carry it as a complete system across every section (C5 travel, C8 digital). **Classification: COMMON (concept-in-open); TEAM-SPECIFIC (complete systems).**
- C5: "handa ka na ba sa biyahe natin ngayon?" → "Una sa ating ruta…" → "red light muna" → "Nakarating na ang ating biyahe sa araw na ito".
- C8: "Tama muna pag-scroll" → "Huwag mag-lag" → unison "Offline!".
- Failures: C4's "walang pinapanigan" (3× in 60 s, never again); C10/C14 announce a mission/tagline that never returns.
**Purpose:** a concept is a system when it gives language for the whole broadcast and a sound for its edges; it is a gimmick when it gives one good line for the first 30 seconds (Specialist L §6). Strength: HIGH.

## 3. Headline Patterns

### P13. Stinger/beep-separated headline rundown, anchors alternating, 2–4 items
**Count:** 18/18 run a headline rundown; 16/18 place an audio separator between items (C6 and C11 are the exceptions — C11 runs four headlines with one closing stinger). Item counts: 2 in 9 entries, 3 in 3, 4 in 5 (+ C2's 4 slots). **Classification: NEAR-UNIVERSAL (rundown); NEAR-UNIVERSAL (separator, 16/18).**
- C4: four headlines, `[HEADLINE STINGER 1–4]` after each, ~13 s total.
- C18: three beeps in ~10 s ("ATP Challenger 50 Fujairah Open, naudlot! Pilipinong tennis player Francis Alcantara, hindi natuloy!").
**Purpose:** the per-item stinger is an "auditory comma" — it tells the listener "new item now" so the anchors need no connective words (Specialist D §5). Strength: HIGH.

### P14. Headline syntax: TOPIC, RESULT-STATE + "!"
**Count:** 17/18 use the "topic phrase, comma, passive/result verb, exclamation" shape for at least one headline; exclamation-mark terminal punctuation in 13/18; 8–14 words is the modal length. **Classification: NEAR-UNIVERSAL.**
- C16: "Iligal na pagbebenta ng financial accounts, nabisto ng pulisya!"
- C18: "Nakalalasong likido na natagpuan sa Spratly Islands, pinakawalan ng mga mangingisdang Tsino!"
- Verb families recur: *arestado/timbog/nasikop* (arrest), *umakyat/pumalo/umigting* (crisis), *naudlot/hindi natuloy/pumalpak* (failure).
**Purpose:** the passive-result verb front-loads the new fact within the listener's ~2–3 s attention window; active verbs are reserved for institutions (Specialist D §3.1–3.2). Strength: HIGH.

### P15. Reveal the hard news, tease the soft news
**Count:** reveal-dominant rundowns in 14/18 (the headline states the outcome; the story adds who/when/law). Question-form teases are used for sports/showbiz in 6/18 (C5, C13, C16 fully; C1, C7, C14 partially) and **never** for hard news. **Classification: COMMON (reveal-dominant); OPTIONAL (question-form soft teases).**
- C5: "sino-sinong artist at anong mga pelikula kaya ang waging sa Oscars 2026?" (question) vs "global stock market pinakangang bahagpumapapa" (reveal).
- C16 mid-break: "Ano-anong films kaya ang nakakuha ng maraming awards…?"
**Purpose:** hard news must be *revealed* (the fact is the product); soft news can be *withheld* (curiosity is the product) — a 4-item rundown that reveals 1–2 and teases 3–4 gets credibility plus a listen-on hook (Specialist D §4.3). Strength: HIGH.

### P16. "kaya" speculative question as a curiosity engine
**Count:** 6/18 (C5, C6, C13, C14, C16, C17). **Classification: OPTIONAL.**
- C6: "Ang tanong ng marami: ano kaya ang epekto nito?"
- C14: "Kumusta na kaya ang mga atletang apektado sa gyara [giyera] sa Middle East?"
**Purpose:** the particle converts the audience from observer to co-investigator — the upcoming segment answers a question the listener now wants answered (Specialist C §8.2). Strength: HIGH.

### P17. Frame line + handoff line around the rundown
**Count:** ~half of the entries bracket the block (frame before item 1, launch line after): C1, C4, C5, C10, C14, C16, C17 (7/18 with both or one strong variant). **Classification: OPTIONAL.**
- C14: "Sa ratsada ng mga balita:" (frame) … "Isyung napapanahon! Rundown ng mga balita! Sa NSPC Patrol, aming ihahatid!" (seal).
- C17: "Simulan na ang balitaan!" (launch line).
**Purpose:** frame + handoff make a staccato list feel like a program; entries with neither (C3, C11, C18) read as bare lists (Specialist D §6). Strength: HIGH.

## 4. News-Writing Patterns

### P18. The five-slot report anatomy: LEAD → SOURCE → ESSENTIAL DETAIL → IMPACT → SIGN-OFF
**Count:** 38 of 41 hard-news reports. **Classification: NEAR-UNIVERSAL.**
- C1 national: "Inatasan ng National Security Council ang Armed Forces at Coast Guard…" (lead) → "Ayon sa NSC…" (source) → "…sampung bote ng cyanide…" (detail) → "plano ng NSC na magsumite ng ulat sa Department of Foreign Affairs…" (impact) → "Des Degala, ulat sa NSPC Patrol."
- Order is invariant: identity (alias, age) follows the lead, statutes land in sentences 2–3, public advisories close before the sign-off (Specialist E §2, §6).
**Purpose:** hook first, verify second, protect the listener last — radio psychology in a 5-minute constraint. Strength: HIGH.

### P19. Action/arrest-first leads with the actor attached to the verb
**Count:** 12/41 reports open with a passive action verb + agent; data-first leads (numbers) in 7/41; impact-first in 4/41; warning-first in 4/41; broken fragments in 4/41. **Classification: COMMON (action-first); the fragment lead is a recurring failure (see F1).**
- C1: "Timbog sa ikinasang operasyon ng Ormoc City Cyber Response Team ang isang scammer…"
- C18: "Matagumpay na nahuli ng Ormoc City Cyber Response Team ang 29-anyos na lalaki…"
**Purpose:** who-did-what in the first three seconds; the strongest leads in the corpus are action-first, the weakest are fragments with no subject for several seconds (Specialist E §4). Strength: HIGH.

### P20. Second-breath attribution ("Ayon sa…")
**Count:** 11/18 name at least one agency or official; the signature line "Ayon sa National Security Council" appears in 5 entries (C1, C6, C9, C12, C17); named-person sourcing (Cornelio Valencia, Juniper Malinao, Donald Trump) in 8 reports. **Classification: COMMON.**
- C7: "Dagdag pa ni Cornelio Valencia, ang paggamit ng cyanide ay isang sabotahe…"
- C8: "ayon kay Juniper Malinao ng National Bureau of Investigation" — the corpus's only fully-sourced report.
**Purpose:** institutional attribution is the credibility floor; named persons are the corpus's clearest marker of script maturity (Specialist E §5). Strength: HIGH. (Correlate: 8/41 reports carry no attribution at all — see F-section.)

### P21. 3-sentence report, 25–35 s of uninterrupted speech
**Count:** median 3 sentences (16 of 41 reports); the standard reporter block runs ~24–31 s; reporter solo speech ≈20–35 s. **Classification: COMMON.**
- C16 local: 3 sentences, ~26 s, six facts, zero corrections.
- C10 national: 2 sentences, ~38 s — the density champion and the most demanding listen.
**Purpose:** 3 sentences fits the 5-minute budget; blocks over ~35 s need a Q&A split or co-anchor to survive (Specialist E §8; Specialist K §3.2). Strength: HIGH.

### P22. Statute-level legal precision in the crime story
**Count:** 13/18 cite the law by name in the local story (RA 12010 / Anti-Financial Account Scamming Act in C1, C4, C6, C7, C8, C9, C11, C12, C13, C14, C15, C16, C18; Cybercrime Prevention Act of 2012 in many of the same). **Classification: COMMON (near-universal within the shared story).**
- C12: "Nilabag nito ang Section 5 of Republic Act 12010 o ang Anti-Financial Account Scamming."
- C13: "Anti-Financial Account Scamming Act… RA 12010 at Cybercrime Prevention Act of 2012… RA 10175."
**Purpose:** one concrete statute makes a 30-second local story feel researched — specificity reads as preparation (Specialist E §3.1; per-script-11 §23). Strength: HIGH.

### P23. Hedged allegations ("umano", "diumano", "daw")
**Count:** 4/18 use hedging correctly in the WPS story (C1, C8, C17, C18); C5 asserts "sabotahe galing sa China" unhedged — the corpus's worst sourcing lapse. **Classification: OPTIONAL (but a strong marker of quality when present).**
- C17: "maituturing umanong pansasabotahe ng China…"
- C18: "ang cyanide na pinaniniwalaang ibinuhos umano ng mga mangingisdang Tsino…"
**Purpose:** legal-safe register for allegations; absence of hedging on an accusation is a measurable quality failure (Specialist E §5). Strength: HIGH.

### P24. Named reporter sign-off on every report ("[Name], para sa [station/beat]")
**Count:** 32 of 34 reporter-delivered reports end with a named sign-off. Formula families: name + station ("Des Degala, ulat sa NSPC Patrol" C1; "Erika Cadungog, para sa NSPC Patrol!" C7), name + beat persona ("Nakatutok sa balita, Ian Rama!" C18; "Nagpapatrol, Oliver Villanueva" C6), name + patriotic tag ("Dain Ryan, Patrol ng Bayan" C13; "Para sa bayang sinilangan, Joshua Casilla!" C16), hand-back ("Balik sa iyo, Dwayne" C12). **Classification: NEAR-UNIVERSAL.**
**Purpose:** the sign-off is the reporter's brand beat and the anchor's cue to re-take the mic; the two strongest families are beat-persona (re-anchors the segment role) and station-tagline fusion (turns the sign-off into branding, C17) (Specialist E §7). Strength: HIGH.

### P25. Public-service advisory as the closing fact
**Count:** ~10/18 local stories end with a listener-facing advisory before the sign-off (C1, C4, C6, C7, C8, C12, C13, C15, C16, C18). **Classification: COMMON.**
- C16: "Hinihikayat naman ng awtoridad ang publiko na mag-report agad kung makararanas ng ganitong transaksyon online."
- C12: "inaanyahanan na ng otoridad ang publiko na maging wais sa online transactions."
**Purpose:** the listener always gets a takeaway before the name; the report "ends with the listener, not with the event" (Specialist E §6, §11). Strength: HIGH.

## 5. Anchor & Chemistry Patterns

### P26. The two-anchor duo (lead + co-anchor)
**Count:** 18/18 — no entry uses a solo anchor; no entry uses more than two regular anchors. **Classification: NEAR-UNIVERSAL.**
**Purpose:** the pair is the structural spine — they bookend the show, chain the headlines, and own every transition (Specialist C §1). Strength: HIGH.

### P27. Paired self-introduction ("Ako si X… at ako naman si Y")
**Count:** 17/18 (absent only in C3, where names are never given). **Classification: NEAR-UNIVERSAL.**
- C6: "Ako po si Kiko Yabao…" → "At ako naman si Loubhel Dalmaso."
- C18: "Ako ang inyong lingkod, Gia Ruzano." → "Ako naman si Ron Cenia."
**Purpose:** the mirrored structure signals "we are one unit" and guarantees both anchors an early speaking turn — a fairness mechanism as much as branding (Specialist C §2.1). Strength: HIGH.

### P28. Question-form handoff to reporters ("[Name], ano ang…?")
**Count:** 12/18 (C1, C2, C3, C5, C7, C8, C9, C12, C13, C14, C15, C17). The showbiz variant has a canonical wording — "ano ang chika?" / "ano ang latest?" / "what's the scoop?" (C1, C3, C14, C15, C17). **Classification: COMMON (near the NEAR-UNIVERSAL boundary).**
- C1: "Ah, Janine, may nahuli na nga ba?"
- C17: "Zal Ramos, ano ang latest?"
**Purpose:** the question pre-loads the reporter's first sentence, eliminates "report-start" hesitation, and makes the entry feel like an interview (Specialist C §5.2). Strength: HIGH.

### P29. Anchor mid-report follow-up question (simulated live two-way)
**Count:** 8/18 (C1, C2, C4, C7, C8, C13, C14, C16 — see Resolution 2). **Classification: COMMON.**
- C13: "Nasaan na ngayon ang sospek?" → "…nasa ilalim na ng kustodiya ng kapulisan si Alias Amboy."
- C16: "Ron, ano na kaya ang lagay ng giyera sa Middle East?" → "Louie, ayon kay US President Donald Trump…"
**Purpose:** the format's strongest realism device — a pre-written report sounds discovered live, and the question adds a second sourced fact (Specialist C §5.4). Works because the questions are generic enough to have been pre-answered. Strength: HIGH.

### P30. Thank-you receipt after the reporter's sign-off
**Count:** 11/18 (C1, C2, C4, C6, C7, C8, C12, C13, C14, C16, C17). **Classification: COMMON.**

## 7. Sound-Design Patterns

### P39. One music bed per category (category identification by sound)
**Count:** 18/18 label distinct beds for news / local-field / sports / showbiz / infomercial / closing; bed counts range 6–11 per entry (mean ≈ 8). **Classification: NEAR-UNIVERSAL.**
- C18: `[FAST SPORTS DRUM BED]` vs `[LIVELY SHOWBIZ CHIKA BED]` vs `[EDITORIAL / COMMENTARY BED — INSPIRATIONAL & SOLEMN]`.
**Purpose:** the listener "hears" the genre before a word is spoken — beds are the primary category-identification device (Specialist G §1, §5). Strength: HIGH.

### P40. Beds duck under speech ("UP AND UNDER" / "DUCKS UNDER SPEECH")
**Count:** ≥16/18 explicitly (C1, C2, C3, C4, C5, C6, C7, C8, C9, C10, C12, C13, C14, C15, C17, C18). **Classification: NEAR-UNIVERSAL.**

**Purpose:** the single most repeated production phrase in the corpus and the primary anti-crowding mechanism — music present, never competing (Specialist G §6). Strength: HIGH.

### P41. Boundary-only stinger placement (stingers at edges, never mid-report)
**Count:** 18/18 in design — stingers land at segment boundaries, headline boundaries, reporter sign-offs, and bumpers; reporter blocks run 20–40 s with zero internal SFX. **Classification: NEAR-UNIVERSAL.**
- C1 national report 04:51–05:22 has no SFX hit.
**Purpose:** sound marks structure, not sentences; restraint is what makes the stingers land (Specialist G §1, §12). Strength: HIGH.

### P42. Sonic bookend (same ID/theme at open and close)
**Count:** 13/18 pair an opening ID with a closing ID/chorus (C1, C2, C4, C6, C7, C8, C9, C12, C13, C14, C15, C17, C18); re-entry stingers act as a compressed mid-broadcast logo in C6, C10, C13, C18. **Classification: COMMON.**
- C5: the sung theme opens and its bed swells again at the close.
- C15: vocal station theme "Sa bawat pagsikat ng araw…" opens; `[OUTRO THEME BED SWELLS & FADE]` closes.
**Purpose:** a bookended sonic identity is the corpus's most disciplined branding device — it appears at exactly the structural points and is never looped inside segments (Specialist G §7). Strength: HIGH.

### P43. Crescendo-and-fade ending
**Count:** all 18 end with a music fade; ~8/18 script an explicit swell-to-climax before the fade (C1, C3, C4, C7, C8, C12, C14, C18). **Classification: COMMON (fade is universal; the crescendo build is OPTIONAL).**
- C7: "FULL CLOSING THEME MUSIC SWELLS TO MAXIMUM CRESCENDO AND FADES OUT SMOOTHLY INTO SILENCE."
**Purpose:** the musical climax is a completion signal that lets the anchors sign off over a rising bed; the fade to `[STUDIO SILENCE]` is itself a designed end (Specialist G §6). Strength: HIGH.

### P44. Vocal/unison stingers (voice-as-SFX)
**Count:** 6/18 (C1 "Mabuhay ka!"; C6 "Eksklusibo!" and VO "Showbiz!"; C8 "Yas Queen!" + "Offline!"; C13 "LABAN, PILIPINAS!"; C15 "Pogi!"; C9's choral "Kasama Ka"). **Classification: OPTIONAL.**

### P45. Dialogue-integrated / theatrical SFX confined to the infomercial zone

## 14. What This Means (short synthesis)

1. **There IS one National formula, but it is an architecture, not a style.** The near-universal set (P1–P7, P13–P14, P18, P24, P26–P27, P35, P38–P41, P46–P48, P52, P56) is a 5-minute skeleton: branded open → stingered headlines → 2–3 compressed reports → promise → one dramatized infomercial → re-entry → soft block → unison close, with category beds ducked under speech and every boundary stinger-marked. A Division-level script that follows this skeleton is *expected*; it will not differentiate on structure alone.
2. **The genuine differentiators are OPTIONAL patterns executed well:** question-form handoffs (P28), mid-report Q&A (P29), role-titled IDs (P9), slogans on a schedule (P10), a carried concept (P12), "X o Y" glossing (P58), one-word punches (P60), and clean delivery (the absence of F1–F2 is itself the strongest differentiator — C10 and C16 prove it).
3. **The dominant shared weakness is execution, not design:** 16/18 entries garble on air, 8/18 mispronounce their own brand, and the corpus's two weakest entries (C2, C12) fail at exactly the points where the near-universal conventions demand precision (IDs, headlines, sign-offs). Rehearsal of the skeleton beats invention of new structure.
4. **TEAM-SPECIFIC devices (C5's trip, C8's Offline!, C17's Tatak-, C6's tandem, C13's chant) are the corpus's most memorable moments — and the one thing a Division script must never copy.** The principle behind each (a concept that gives language to every seam and a sound to its edges; a closing that answers the opening) is the reusable lesson.
