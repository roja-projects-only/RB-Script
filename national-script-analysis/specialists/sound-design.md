# Specialist G — Sound Design: How National-Level (NSPC) Radio Scripts Use Sound

**Corpus:** 18 NSPC Radio Broadcasting & Scriptwriting entries, `transcripts/Contestant-1..18/actual-script.md` (all Verified per `corpus-inventory.md`; mic-check phases excluded). Broadcast windows run ~04:40–05:27 (280–327 s).

**Evidence discipline:** every claim below is labeled **DIRECTLY OBSERVED** (quoted cue labels from the scripts), **CROSS-SCRIPT PATTERN** (seen in ≥3 entries, named), or **ANALYST INFERENCE** (interpretation, reasoning given). Music/SFX are *documented cue labels, not recordings* (corpus-inventory §5.6) — this report analyzes the scripts' sound *design intent*, not the audio itself.

**Counting method (analyst):** "SFX event" = one discrete, non-music sound cue (stinger hit, beep, chime, bell, ambience bed, phone filter, whistle, applause, spoken stinger). Music-bed starts/swells/ducks are tracked separately as "bed movements" because they are level gestures, not sounds. Rates are computed from broadcast-window durations in `corpus-inventory.md`; they are analyst arithmetic on observed cues, so treat ±0.2/min tolerance.

## 1. The Shared National Sound Architecture (CROSS-SCRIPT PATTERN)

All 18 entries run the same skeleton: **sonic open → station ID → news (category beds) → infomercial (sound-rich zone) → sports → showbiz → crescendo close**. Within that skeleton, sound is assigned consistent jobs:

1. **Sound marks structure, not sentences.** Stingers land at segment boundaries, headline boundaries, reporter sign-offs, and bumpers — almost never inside a news report's body.
2. **Music beds do category identification.** Every entry distinguishes news / local-field / sports / showbiz / infomercial / closing with separate bed labels; the music tells the listener which genre is coming before a word is spoken.
3. **Beds duck under speech.** "UP AND UNDER" / "DUCKS UNDER SPEECH" appears in ≥16 of 18 entries (explicit in C1, C2, C3, C4, C5, C6, C7, C8, C9, C10, C12, C13, C14, C15, C17, C18).
4. **The infomercial is the designated sound-rich zone.** Drama SFX (keyboards, bells, thunder, game-show jingles) cluster in the advocacy segment, keeping the news sections sonically clean.
5. **Voice is used as SFX.** Spoken unison lines and vocal stingers ("Offline!", "Yas Queen!", "Pogi!", "Eksklusibo!") do the work of stingers at zero production cost.

## 3. SFX Frequency: Density Spectrum

**CROSS-SCRIPT PATTERN** — the corpus splits into three density bands:

- **Sparse band (≤1.9/min): C1, C6, C10, C11, C12, C13, C16, C18** (8 entries). These trust beds + a handful of boundary stingers. C10 and C16 are the cleanest demonstrations: ~8–9 SFX events in ~5 minutes, zero mid-report hits, and no loss of cohesion.
- **Moderate band (2.0–2.9/min): C4, C5, C7, C8, C9, C14, C17** (7 entries). Standard radio-competition density: stinger per headline, stinger at each reporter sign-off, bumpers at the break.
- **Dense band (≥3.0/min): C2, C3** (2 entries). Both cross 3.5/min. Notably, both are dense for *different* reasons — C2 through per-headline stinger variety, C3 through the dramatized AI infomercial — and both sit in the weakest halves of the corpus for spoken accuracy (many `[unclear]`/correction tags).

**ANALYST INFERENCE:** the density correlation is not causal (C12 is sparse but degraded), but it is meaningful that the two densest entries are also the two whose scripts show the most audio clutter around dialogue. Dense stinger use does not rescue weak delivery; it competes with it.

| Vocal stingers | `TEAM / STINGER: Yas Queen!` (C8), `BUMPER VOICE: Bogie! [Pogi!]` (C15), spoken "Eksklusibo!" (C6), "Showbiz!" (C6), unison "Offline!" (C8), "LABAN PILIPINAS!" (C13), "Mabuhay ka!" (C1) | Branding + emphasis + comedy |
| Rare one-offs | `SIGN-OFF CHORAL STINGER / APPLAUSE` (C9) | Closure celebration (unique in corpus) |

**Placement rules (CROSS-SCRIPT PATTERN):**

1. **Headline boundaries:** one stinger per headline (C2, C4, C5, C12, C13, C18) or accents between headline blocks (C3, C7, C9, C10, C14, C15, C16). C11 is the exception: all four headlines run *unpunctuated*, with a single `HEADLINE STINGER ACCENT / TRANSITION INTO NEWS BED` after the last one.
2. **Reporter sign-offs:** stinger at the moment of sign-off or hand-back — e.g., C5 `[SFX: STINGER]` immediately before "Citi Aziz at [NSPC] Patrol"; C9 `[SPORTS OUTRO STINGER]` with "Sunny Cedillo!"; C13 `[AUDIO: SHOWBIZ CHIKA BED]` before "Rain Calzo, show this patroller!".
3. **Bumpers:** break-in and return each get a stinger/bumper pair, creating a symmetrical ad-break frame — C1 `[COMMERCIAL BREAK STINGER]` (05:41) / `[STATION STINGER / RETURN FROM BREAK]` (06:25); C6 `[COMMERCIAL BUMPER / STINGER]` (05:43) / `[STATION STINGER / NEWS BED RE-ENTRY]` (06:22); C13 `[TEASER STINGER / COMMERCIAL BUMPER BED]` (05:09) / `[STATION RETURN STINGER]` (06:08).
4. **Station ID moments:** the ID voice/chorus is always accompanied by (or immediately followed by) a stinger — C4 `[SFX: HEAVY DRUM HIT / PROGRAM STINGER]` + "NSPC Patrol!"; C7 drum roll → "Ito ang…" → `[STINGER ACCENT]` → "DZJV-9126!" → "SPC Patrol!"; C8 `[SIGNATURE STATION ID STINGER]`.

**ANALYST INFERENCE:** the headline stinger works as an *auditory comma* — it tells the listener "new item now" so the anchors do not have to say it. Entries with per-headline stingers (C4, C12, C13) also run terse one-line headlines; the stinger substitutes for connective words. C11's unpunctuated run shows the cost: four headlines blur together without the audible comma.

## 5. Music Bed Strategy

**How many beds (DIRECTLY OBSERVED, from rundown tables):** 6–11 distinct bed labels per entry (mean ≈ 8). The most bed-prolific entries are C13 (11: headline intro, station jingle, news, national, field, infomercial drama, advocacy, local, live report, showbiz, outro) and C18 (10), both of which segment their rundown finely.

**Category differentiation (CROSS-SCRIPT PATTERN, 18/18):** every entry labels a distinct bed for at least: news/main, sports, showbiz, infomercial/advocacy, and closing/outro. Explicit genre adjectives appear in the labels themselves:

- C4: `[SPORTS BED — HIGH ENERGY ROCK / SYNTH BEAT]` vs `[SHOWBIZ BED — GLAMOROUS / UPBEAT POP BED]` vs `[CASUAL TALK BED SWELLS UNDER ANCHORS]` for closing banter.
- C3: `[GLAMOUR / SHOWBIZ BED]` vs `[DYNAMIC SPORTS BED]` vs `[OMINOUS BED]` (infomercial).
- C18: `[FAST SPORTS DRUM BED]` vs `[LIVELY SHOWBIZ CHIKA BED]` vs `[EDITORIAL / COMMENTARY BED — INSPIRATIONAL & SOLEMN TONE]`.
- C16: `[SHOWBIZ STINGER / UPBEAT POP BED]`.

**ANALYST INFERENCE on function:** beds are the *primary* category-identification device. Because the music changes at the same moment the anchor says the category name ("Para sa sports…", "Buzzing Showbiz…"), the two signals reinforce each other; the listener gets the genre cue twice. This is why entries can keep SFX counts low (C10, C16) without losing orientation.

**Bed movements (CROSS-SCRIPT PATTERN):** the standard lifecycle is **swell-in → duck under speech → hold under → swell-out/stop**, with `UP AND UNDER` the most repeated production phrase in the corpus (explicit in ≥12 entries: C1, C2, C4, C5, C6, C7, C9, C10, C13, C14, C15, C17, C18). Bed *changes* double as transitions: C5 `[MUSIC BUMPER TRANSITION — NEWS BED RISES THEN SETTLES UNDER]`; C8 `[SHOWBIZ THEME CROSSFADES INTO CLOSING THEME]`; C14 `[SPORTS BED DUCKS AND ACCENTS OUT WITH STINGER]`.

**One sub-brand bed stands out (DIRECTLY OBSERVED):** C15 creates a named sub-format, "NSPC Patrol Special Updates" (stinger voice 03:52 and 04:37), with its own bed — a second sonic layer *inside* the program. No other entry builds a recurring sub-branded sound.

## 6. Fades: Up-and-Under, Crescendo, Fade-Out

**Up-and-under (CROSS-SCRIPT PATTERN, ≥16/18):** the single most important anti-crowding mechanism. Quoted examples: C1 `[NEWS THEME BED UP AND UNDER]` (02:53); C4 `[NEWS BED SWELLS THEN DUCKS UNDER VO]` (04:17); C7 `[THEME CROSSFADES SMOOTHLY INTO UPTEMPO NEWS BED - VOLUME DUCKS UNDER SPEECH]` (03:03); C12 `[NEWS BED SWELLS THEN DUCKS UNDER SPEECH]` (03:25); C18 `[NATIONAL NEWS BED SWELLS BRIEFLY, THEN DUCKS UNDER SPEECH]` (03:58).

**Crescendo endings (CROSS-SCRIPT PATTERN, ~8/18 explicit):** C1 `[OUTRO MUSIC CRESCENDO & FADE OUT]`; C3 `[THEME MUSIC CLIMAX AND FADES OUT]`; C4 `[OUTRO THEME SWELLS TO CRESCENDO AND FADES OUT COMPLETELY]`; C7 `[FULL CLOSING THEME MUSIC SWELLS TO MAXIMUM CRESCENDO AND FADES OUT SMOOTHLY INTO SILENCE]`; C12 `[CLOSING THEME SWELLS TO CLIMAX AND FADES OUT]`; C14 `[THEME MUSIC RISES TO FULL CRESCENDO, FINAL HIT ACCENT, AND FADES OUT]`; C18 `[CLOSING THEME MUSIC SWELLS TO CLIMAX AND FADES OUT]`; C8 `[THEME MUSIC HITS FINAL RESOLUTION CHORD AND DUCKS OUT]`.

**Mid-show swells:** C4 `[MUSIC SWELLS TO INSPIRING STRINGS, THEN LOWERS UNDER VOICEOVER]` (infomercial turn); C5 `[SFX: DRAMATIC CHORD / MUSIC SWELLS]` (PSA turn); C13 `[ADVOCACY MUSIC BED SWELLS]`; C14 `[INFOMERCIAL MUSIC SWELLS AND RESOLVES]`.

**ANALYST INFERENCE:** the swell-into-climax close is a *completion signal*: it tells the listener (and the judges) the broadcast is ending, which lets the anchors deliver sign-off lines over a rising bed instead of needing "and now we close." The fade to silence that follows is itself a designed sound — the broadcast ends, then the room tone/`[STUDIO SILENCE]` is explicitly noted in C4, C9, C13, C15, C17 (e.g., C15 `[AUDIO: STATION THEME FADES OUT COMPLETELY] [STUDIO SILENCE]`).

- **C1:** station ID voice "Tawag Pilipino, Serapisya…" over `[STATION STINGER]`; chorus "DZJV 91.26 NSPC Patrol!" at 07:46 over `[OUTRO MUSIC CRESCENDO]`.

- **C9:** choral jingle "…Dito sa NSPC Patrol, kasama ka" (02:56–03:22); sign-off "Muli sa NSPC Patrol, Kasama Ka!" over `[SIGN-OFF CHORAL STINGER / APPLAUSE]`.

## 8. Category Sounds: Clocks, Beeps, and Genre Accents

### 8.1 Time check (clock sound)
**DIRECTLY OBSERVED:** beep/chime time checks in **8/18** — C1 `[SFX: TIME CHECK BEEP]` ("Oras ngayon na alauna ng hapon"), C2 `[SFX: TIME CHECK BEEP / CHIME]`, C3 `[SFX: TIME CHECK BEEP]`, C4 `[SFX: TIME CHECK CHIME / BEEP]`, C6 `[SFX: TIME CHECK BEEP / CHIME]` at the very top, C11 `[TIME CHECK CHIME / STATION RE-ENTRY STINGER]`, C14 `[TIME CHIME]`, C9 `[TIME CHECK BUMPER]`. **Spoken-only** in 6 (C5, C10, C13, C15, C16, C18). **Absent** in 4 (C7, C8, C12, C17).

**Function (ANALYST INFERENCE):** the beep is the *realism* device — it reproduces the broadcast convention of the hourly time signal ("Oras sa Pilipinas…" is a real Radyo Patrol ritual), and it doubles as an attention reset before the next block. C16 and C18 show the spoken-only alternative is perfectly acceptable; C14's `[TIME CHIME]` at "Ang ating oras, ganap nang alas 3 ng hapon" is the most elegant pairing (formal language + formal chime).

### 8.2 Headline beeps
**DIRECTLY OBSERVED:** CROSS-SCRIPT PATTERN in 11/18 (per-headline: C2, C4, C5, C12, C13, C18; block accents: C3, C7, C9, C10, C14, C15, C16). The labels literally say what they punctuate: `HEADLINE ACCENT BEEP` (C15, C18), `HEADLINE STINGER 1…4` (C4), `HEADLINE STINGER / BEEP` (C16).

**Function:** attention reset + emphasis (auditory comma, §4).

### 8.3 Genre accent SFX
**DIRECTLY OBSERVED:** the strongest examples are C14's `[SFX: SPORTS STINGER / WHISTLE SFX]` (whistle = sports iconography) and `[SFX: SHOWBIZ SPARKLE / GLITZ ACCENT]` (sparkle = entertainment iconography); C6's VO "Showbiz!" + stinger; C2's `[SPORTS THEME ACCENT]` used inside a sports headline; C16's `[SFX: SHOWBIZ STINGER / UPBEAT POP BED]`.

**ANALYST INFERENCE:** genre accents are the *fastest* category-identification channel — a whistle is read as "sports" in under a second, before the anchor finishes the lead. But they only work if the iconography is culturally legible (whistle→sports, sparkle→glamour); C2's generic `SPORTS THEME ACCENT` label does not tell the operator what sound to play, weakening the cue (see §11).

## 9. Silence & Pauses

**DIRECTLY OBSERVED — scripted silence is rare and always purposeful:**

1. **Comic beat:** C2 `[SFX: CLASSROOM SILENCE]` (05:43) immediately before the students' deadpan "Wala." — the only mid-show silence in the corpus, and it is a punchline setup.
2. **Dramatic build:** C7's drum roll with the pause inside the ID ("Ito ang…" … `[STINGER ACCENT]` … "DZJV-9126!") — silence-as-suspense before the logo.
3. **End-of-broadcast silence:** documented in C4 (`FADES OUT COMPLETELY`), C9 (`OUTRO MUSIC FADES OUT / AMBIENCE / ROOM SILENCE`), C13 (`STUDIO ROOM TONE / END OF BROADCAST`), C15 (`[STUDIO SILENCE]`), C17 (`STUDIO ROOM TONE / RECORDING ENDS`).
4. **Bed fade-under as pseudo-silence:** C3 `[OMINOUS BED FADES UNDER NARRATOR]` creates a hush moment before the PSA narrator's moral.

**CROSS-SCRIPT PATTERN:** silence is used at the *macro* level (ending) and the *micro* level (one comic beat), never mid-news. No entry scripts dead air inside a news report.

**ANALYST INFERENCE:** the corpus treats silence as a high-value commodity — the absence of sound inside reports makes the one scripted silence (C2) land hard. Teams that fill every gap with stingers (C2, C3) blunt exactly this effect.

## 10. Dialogue-Integrated SFX & Voice-as-SFX

**Dialogue-integrated SFX (DIRECTLY OBSERVED):**

- **C3:** the AI infomercial is a sound-effects scene: `[SFX: FAST KEYBOARD TYPING, PANIC PANTING]` under the student's "Naku, test na bukas"; `[SFX: ROBOTIC POWER-ON CHIME / SYNTHESIS]` for the AI's entrance; `[SFX: DRAMATIC THUNDER / OMINOUS BASS DROP]` at "Hindi!"; and the SFX written *inside* the dialogue line: `[SFX / EVIL LAUGH: Buwaan!]` — the laugh is both a sound effect and a spoken line.
- **C5:** `[SFX: CAR HORN HONK / BEEP BEEP]` is given a spoken rendering as `AUDIO EFFECT: Pipip!` — the script tells the operator to make the horn *say* the word; theme and sound merge.
- **C2:** school bell → teacher scene; phone patch before the live report.
- **C1:** game-show SFX under the pahulaan Q&A ("isa, dalawa, tatlo, hulaan 'yan!").
- **C18:** `[PLAYFUL GAME SHOW / UPTEMPO INFOMERCIAL JINGLE]` under the DepEd quiz skit.

**Voice-as-SFX (DIRECTLY OBSERVED):** C6 "Eksklusibo!" (spoken immediately after `[SFX: BREAKING NEWS STINGER]`); C8 `[TEAM / STINGER: Yas Queen!]` — the team's chant is the stinger, and unison "Offline!" closes the show; C15 `BUMPER VOICE: Bogie! [Pogi!]`; unison sign-offs C1 "Mabuhay ka!", C13 "LABAN PILIPINAS!", C9 "Kasama Ka!", C18 "Mabuhay ang magiting na radyo peryodistang Pilipino".

**ANALYST INFERENCE:** dialogue-integrated SFX is the corpus's clearest evidence of *designed* sound — these are not background fills but scripted characters (the horn that says "Pipip!", the AI that laughs). The function is comedy/realism inside the infomercial zone and branding at the sign-off. The risk is that these moments are the hardest to execute live; C3's evil-laugh cue shows how quickly a theatrical SFX can tip into gimmick.

## 12. How National Scripts Use Sound WITHOUT Overcrowding Dialogue

**CROSS-SCRIPT PATTERN — five mechanisms:**

1. **Boundary-only placement.** Stingers occur at segment edges, headline edges, sign-offs, and bumpers — never mid-report. Reporter blocks run 20–40 s of unbroken bed-under speech (e.g., C1 national report 04:51–05:22 with zero SFX hits; C16 world report 04:16–04:36).
2. **Beds replace words.** Category beds (and genre accents) carry orientation, so anchors never need to say "now switching to sports" — the music says it (C4, C14, C18 labels prove the intent).
3. **Up-and-under as the default level law.** `UP AND UNDER` / `DUCKS UNDER SPEECH` in ≥16/18 entries guarantees music never fights dialogue; the swell-in at each new bed also re-energizes pacing without a single extra SFX.
4. **Concentrated sound zones.** The infomercial absorbs nearly all theatrical SFX (C1 game show, C2 bell/silence, C3 keyboard/thunder/laugh, C5 traffic, C18 game show). News segments stay sparse; the contrast makes the infomercial feel like a different program — which is the point of a break.
5. **Restraint as a deliberate option.** C10 (1.9/min), C11 (1.6), C16 (1.5), C15 (1.0) demonstrate that National scripts do not require high SFX rates. C15 delivers the whole broadcast with 5 SFX events, relying on its vocal jingle, the Special Updates sub-bumper, and headline beeps — and is among the most branded entries in the corpus.

## 13. Critical Findings: Overuse, Cue Confusion, and Weaknesses

1. **Density overkill in C2 and C3 (3.6/min).** C2 fires 17 SFX events in 286 s, including stingers *between* an anchor's thanks and the next beat (`[SFX: STINGER]` 05:23 inside "Maghansalamat, Mica." exchange). C3 packs 4 SFX into the 30-second AI scene. **ANALYST INFERENCE:** at >3/min, stingers stop punctuating and start competing; both entries also carry the corpus's heaviest `[unclear]`/correction loads, suggesting sound clutter tracked with delivery strain.

2. **Generic stinger labels create operator ambiguity (CROSS-SCRIPT PATTERN, ~12/18).** Labels like `[STINGER]` (C1, C4, C5, C13, C17), `[STINGER ACCENT]`, `[NEWS BED STINGER]` (C1), `[TRANSITION STINGER]` (C3, C6, C7, C8, C16, C18) do not tell the audio operator *which* stinger to play. Contrast C14's self-describing labels (`SPORTS STINGER / WHISTLE`, `SHOWBIZ SPARKLE / GLITZ ACCENT`) — a script-readability win (see also Section 20 of per-script analyses). **DIRECTLY OBSERVED** label confusion: C3 `[AUDIO CUE: SHOWBIZ STINGER - DYNAMIC SPORTS BED STARTS]` names the *next segment's* genre, not the sound; `[AUDIO CUE: SPORTS STINGER - CLOSING THEME BED RISES]` likewise. A "showbiz stinger" that leads into sports is a cue the operator cannot execute literally.

3. **Stinger stacking (C7).** `[AUDIO: DRAMATIC DRUM ROLL / IMPACT STINGER]` (02:58) + `[AUDIO: STINGER ACCENT]` (02:59) fire two impacts within one second before the ID. **ANALYST INFERENCE:** a double-hit can read as a technical flub rather than an accent.

4. **Headline-run punctuation gaps.** C11 runs four headlines with a single stinger after the fact; C17 uses one `HEADLINE BED / BEEP SFX` for two headlines. The audible comma disappears exactly where the corpus's own convention (11/18) says it belongs. **ANALYST INFERENCE:** these are forgivable but inconsistent; the stinger system is strongest when its rules are uniform (C4's `HEADLINE STINGER 1–4`).

5. **Infomercial as audio placeholder (C8).** The break is scripted only as `[AUDIO: COMMERCIAL BREAK / PSA / DRAMATIZED INFOMERCIAL PRESENTATION]` with no dialogue — the longest unscripted gap in the corpus. **DIRECTLY OBSERVED weakness:** if the recording contains a rehearsed infomercial, the *script* gives the operator and cast nothing to execute; the segment's quality is unverifiable from text.

6. **Theme-SFX mismatch (C5).** The road-trip theme uses `[SFX: VEHICLE ENGINE / TRAFFIC AMBIENCE]` — a *cacophony* sound — under the PSA turn. **ANALYST INFERENCE:** ambience under dialogue is the one sound type the corpus otherwise avoids; C5's traffic bed is the exception that proves the "ducks under speech" rule.

7. **The time-check convention is inconsistently cued.** 8/18 beep it, 6/18 speak it, 4/18 skip it. None of these is wrong — but within a judged competition where teams imitate a shared Radyo Patrol convention, the beep is the audible signal that the ritual is happening; spoken-only versions (C5, C10, C15, C16, C18) lose the reset.

8. **Sonic-identity collision across entries.** Multiple teams use nearly identical ID formulas ("…91.26 NSPC Patrol!" with different first letters: DZJV, DZJB, DCJV, DZJP, DCJT, PCTV, PCJV). **DIRECTLY OBSERVED** at the corpus level (corpus-inventory §4 notes the 91.26 pattern is a creative prop). **ANALYST INFERENCE:** teams differentiated their *sound* (C5's horn, C8's "Offline!", C9's choral "Kasama ka", C15's Special Updates) precisely to stand apart; teams that only swapped the callsign letters (C1, C2, C15 all using the DZ__ 91.26 + chorus pattern) are sonically indistinguishable from each other in script form.

## 14. Reusable Lessons

### KEEP AS A PRINCIPLE
1. **Let beds carry categories, stingers carry boundaries.** Label beds with their genre (news/sports/showbiz/infomercial/closing) and place stingers only at edges — the 18/18 category-bed convention and the boundary-only placement rule are the corpus's two strongest sound-design habits.
2. **Write "UP AND UNDER" into the script.** The duck is the single most repeated production phrase in the corpus (≥16/18) and the primary anti-crowding mechanism; a script that omits it invites music-over-voice.
3. **Punctuate headlines with one audible comma per item** (C4's `HEADLINE STINGER 1–4`; C18's three beeps) or none at all (C10) — but never inconsistently.
4. **Bookend the broadcast sonically:** open and close with the same ID/chorus signature; add one re-entry stinger after the break (C6, C10, C13, C18).
5. **Make SFX self-describing:** `SHOWBIZ SPARKLE / GLITZ ACCENT` (C14) beats `[STINGER]` — the operator should not have to guess which sound file to play.
6. **Close with a swell, climax, and fade to silence** (C4, C7, C14): it is the universal completion signal and costs one music cue.
7. **Concentrate theatrical SFX in the infomercial zone** so news stays clean and the break feels like a different program (C1, C2, C3, C18).

### ADAPT CAREFULLY
8. **Theme-integrated SFX** (C5's horn that says "Pipip!") is memorable but derivative-prone if copied — it must grow from the entry's own concept, not be bolted on.
9. **Voice-as-stinger** ("Eksklusibo!", "Yas Queen!", "Pogi!") is cheap and effective, but each team needs its *own* phrase; the technique is keepable, the words are not.
10. **Sub-branded sound layers** (C15's "Special Updates" bed) add sophistication but eat clock in a 5-minute window; use at most one.
11. **Drama-SFX clusters** (C3's AI scene) work only with flawless delivery — at National level the scripted SFX cannot rescue shaky acting.

### DO NOT COPY
12. No team's station identity: "…91.26 NSPC Patrol" callsign-plus-chorus formulas, sung jingles (C5, C9, C13, C15), sign-off signatures ("Offline!", "LABAN PILIPINAS!", "Kasama Ka!", "Mabuhay ang magiting na radyo peryodistang Pilipino"), or sub-brand names ("NSPC Patrol Special Updates"). Study the *placement logic*, invent the sounds.
