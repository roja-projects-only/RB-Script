# Specialist Report D — Headline Techniques (National-Level NSPC Corpus)

**Scope:** All 18 entries, primary source = each `transcripts/Contestant-N/actual-script.md`. The "headline block" is defined as the rundown of upcoming stories delivered near the top of the broadcast (typically right after or right before station ID/greetings). Mid-broadcast teasers (pre-commercial break) are analyzed separately in section 7.

**Evidence labels:** `DIRECTLY OBSERVED` (quoted from scripts) · `CROSS-SCRIPT PATTERN` (observed in multiple entries, named) · `ANALYST INFERENCE` (interpretation, flagged with reasoning).

**Transcription caveat (carried from corpus-inventory §5):** Entries 2, 12, and parts of 3, 4, 11, 15 contain heavily garbled lines (`[unclear]`, `[possibly: …]`, slips preserved). Where a headline's wording is corrupt, I quote the intended reading given in the transcript brackets and flag it; syntax judgments on those entries are qualified.

## 1. Executive Summary

- The National-level headline block is a **compressed, stinger-punctuated rundown of 2–4 items**, delivered by the two anchors alternating, in **8–14 words per headline**, almost always built on the pattern **TOPIC PHRASE, RESULT STATE + `!`** (`DIRECTLY OBSERVED`, 17/18 entries).
- **Per-item audio separators are near-universal**: 16/18 entries place a stinger/beep between headlines; the two exceptions (C6, C11) are noted in §6 (`CROSS-SCRIPT PATTERN`).
- **Teaser-vs-reveal is a placement system, not a binary**: hard news (national/local) is almost always *revealed* in the rundown; sports and showbiz are the slots where teams switch to *question-form teases* (C5, C13, C16 mid-break) or defer detail ("abangan!") (`CROSS-SCRIPT PATTERN`).
- **Headline count is the weakest variable in the corpus**: 9/18 entries run only **2 headlines** (C1, C3, C7, C8, C9, C10, C15, C16, C17), which under-covers the 4–5 categories the broadcast actually contains (local, national, infomercial, sports, showbiz). The strongest rundowns run 4 (C4, C5, C11, C12, C13).
- **Weak headline sets are identifiable and mostly avoidable**: content-free fragments (C2, C9, C14), same-story duplication inside one rundown (C6), over-long single headlines (C10, C17), and a 2-item rundown that names no sports/showbiz despite both being in the broadcast (C15) (`DIRECTLY OBSERVED`; verdict = `ANALYST INFERENCE`).

## 3. Reverse-Engineered Syntax

### 3.1 The dominant clause shape: TOPIC, STATE (passive/result)

Nearly every declarative headline is a single sentence with a **comma after the topic noun phrase**, then a **result state expressed as a past participle or existential phrase**. Examples (`DIRECTLY OBSERVED`):

- C4: "May kinam-scammer sa Ormoc arestalo!" (intended: "ATM scammer sa Ormoc, arestado!")
- C16: "Iligal na pagbebenta ng financial accounts, nabisto ng pulisya!"
- C18: "Suspek sa iligal na pagbebenta ng financial accounts, nasa kustodiya ng pulisya!"
- C12: "Iska-murder, pinatitong sangkot sa iligat na magbenta ng financial accounts, aarestaro." (intended: "Scammer, sangkot sa iligal na pagbenta ng financial accounts, arestado.")
- C17: "Presyo ng crude oil sa Estados Unidos, umakyat na sa may higit pitumpung dolyar kada bariles."

The spoken comma is a vocal pause — the anchor states *what* (topic) then *what happened* (result). This is the same shape as printed news headlines ("INCIDENT, [VERB]") but spoken.

### 3.2 Verb profile

The verb slot is dominated by **passive past participles / result states** rather than active verbs (`CROSS-SCRIPT PATTERN` across C4, C6, C7, C8, C12, C13, C15, C16, C17, C18):

- **Arrest/confiscation family:** *arestado, timbog, nasikop, huli, kulong, nasabat, nabisto, nakumpiska, nasamsam, natagpuan, nasa kustodiya*
- **Rise/crisis family:** *tumataas, umakyat, pumalo, umabot, lumawak (lumadak), umigting (umire), nagkakulang*
- **Failure/disruption family:** *pumalpak, naudlot, hindi natuloy, kanselado, bigong makuha*
- **Active verbs are rare and reserved for institutions:** *"National Security Council, magsusumite ng ulat"* (C7), *"nagbigay aksyon"* (C12), *"pinakawalan ng mga mangingisdang Tsino"* (C18).

`ANALYST INFERENCE:` The passive-result verb is the correct choice for radio headlines because it front-loads the *new fact* (what changed) and lets the listener infer agency from the topic phrase. An active-voice headline would delay the payoff past the listener's attention window (~2–3 seconds).

### 3.3 Word count

- Typical headline: **8–14 words** of intended text (`CROSS-SCRIPT PATTERN`; table §2).
- Outliers: C4 compresses to **6–8 words**; C10's second headline runs **~20 words** ("Ang 10-araw na extension sa deadline ng pagpapabukas sa Strait of Hormuz ay pinataw ni US President Donald Trump laban sa Iran!") and C17's first runs **~18–20 words** — both are single-breath-maximum or beyond.
- `ANALYST INFERENCE:` the 8–14 range ≈ 4–7 seconds of speech at broadcast pace; 20+ words ≈ 8–10 seconds, which is too long for a rundown item whose separator is 2–6 seconds away.

### 3.4 Urgency & punctuation

- **Exclamation marks are the default terminal punctuation**: 13/18 entries end at least one headline with `!` (C1, C4, C7, C8, C10, C11, C12, C13, C14, C15, C16, C17, C18 — per script text).
- **Urgency words** (opening or embedded): *"mainit-init"* (C1 ×2), *"mainit-init na balita"* (C7), *"masamang balita"* (C6), *"nakakabahala"* (C6), *"Naku"* (C1, C6), *"Eksklusibo!"* (C6, as breaking-news frame), *"tumutok na", "abangan", "alamin na", "tutukan"* (C1, C7, C10, C13).
- **Question marks** appear only in tease-form headlines (§5): C5 (2), C13 (2), C16 mid-break (1).
- `ANALYST INFERENCE:` the exclamation is a *delivery instruction* on paper — it tells the anchor to hit the result word with energy. The corpus never uses a full stop in a headline slot.

### 3.5 Topic order (first-item bias)

- **National/international leads the rundown in 12/18 entries** (C3, C4, C6, C7, C9, C10, C12, C13, C14, C15, C16, C17, C18 — WPS cyanide or Middle-East oil crisis).
- **Local (Ormoc scam) leads in 4/18** (C1, C2, C8, C11).
- **Showbiz and sports, when present, are last** in every entry that includes them (C2, C4, C5, C11, C12, C13, C14) — never first. `CROSS-SCRIPT PATTERN`.
- `ANALYST INFERENCE:` the order encodes *gravity*: hard national news first (seriousness), soft categories last (entertainment as the hook that keeps listeners past the break, since the showbiz/sports stories air after the infomercial in the full broadcast — see §7).

## 4. Teaser-vs-Reveal Strategy

### 4.1 Reveal-dominant entries (state the outcome in the rundown)

`DIRECTLY OBSERVED:` C1 ("ang kaso ng scamming, patuloy na tumataas!"), C3, C4, C6, C7, C8, C9, C10, C12, C14 (items 1–2), C15, C16, C17, C18 — the rundown gives the result; the story block then supplies the details (who, when, law, source). In these entries the headline block functions as a **table of contents with spoilers**.

### 4.2 Tease-dominant entries (withhold the outcome)

- **C5:** showbiz and sports are pure questions — "sino-sinong artist at anong mga pelikula kaya ang waging sa Oscars 2026?" and "ano kaya ang kalagayan ng isang Pilipino tennis player…?" — while hard news items are revealed declaratively. `DIRECTLY OBSERVED`.
- **C13:** 2 of 4 items are questions ("Anong labang aksyon ng pamahalaan sa nagkalag na bote ng skyline sa karagatan ng Pilipinas?", "hapon award kaya ulit ang isang filmmaking company…?") and the other 2 are imperative teases ("alamin na", "tutukan"). `DIRECTLY OBSERVED`.
- **C14 item 3:** "At highlights na resulta ng Academy Awards ngayong taon!" — a *content-free* tease naming no film, no actor, no outcome (see §8 weaknesses).
- **C16 mid-break teaser:** "Ano-anong films kaya ang nakakuha ng maraming awards sa film competition ngayong 2026?" `DIRECTLY OBSERVED`.

### 4.3 The emergent pattern: reveal the hard, tease the soft

`CROSS-SCRIPT PATTERN` (C5, C13, C16, and partially C1, C7, C14): every entry that uses question-form teases applies them to **sports or showbiz**, never to national/local hard news. The reverse — a question-form hard-news tease — appears zero times.

`ANALYST INFERENCE:` this is a sound information design. Hard news must be *revealed* because the listener's takeaway is the fact itself ("arestado", "nakumpiska"); soft news can be *withheld* because its value is curiosity ("who won the Oscar?"). A 4-item rundown that reveals items 1–2 and teases items 3–4 (as C5 does) gets both credibility and a listen-on hook in the same block.

### 4.4 Post-headline "tumutok" block (C1 only)

C1 extends the rundown with a 3-line audience-address tease ("Kaya naman, kahit sub-sub sa traffic… tumutok na! Dahil walang balitang dapat malapasin na! Pilipinas… balitaan na!"). `DIRECTLY OBSERVED`. This converts the rundown into a *call to stay* — a device no other entry uses at that position (`ANALYST INFERENCE:` it works because the anchors enumerate the listener's own situations — traffic, housework — before demanding attention).

## 5. Punctuation of Sound: Separators and Stinger Timing

### 5.1 Separator types

`CROSS-SCRIPT PATTERN` — 16/18 entries place an audio separator between rundown items:

- **Neutral beep:** `[SFX: BEEP]` / `HEADLINE ACCENT BEEP` / `HEADLINE BEEP / STINGER FX` — C1, C7, C15, C16, C18 (also C17's single `HEADLINE BED / BEEP SFX`).
- **Generic accent/stinger:** `HEADLINE STINGER`, `HEADLINE STINGER ACCENT`, `HEADLINE BUMPER ACCENT`, `NEWS ACCENT STINGER` — C3, C4, C8, C9, C10, C12, C13, C14.
- **Genre-labeled accents:** `SPORTS THEME ACCENT` / `SPORTS STINGER ACCENT` (C2, C5), `STINGER/DRUM HIT`, `STINGER/BRASS ACCENT`, `STINGER/SWOOSH` (C2, C5).
- **Exceptions:** C6 has **no labeled separator inside the headline block** (items are separated by anchor turns and interjections only); C11 has a single `HEADLINE STINGER ACCENT` at block end. `DIRECTLY OBSERVED`.

### 5.2 Stinger timing

From the timestamps: item-to-item spacing (stinger-to-stinger) runs **~2.5–6 seconds**; the fastest blocks are C18 (3 stingers in ~10s → one every ~3.3s), C15 (2 beeps in ~10s), C16 (2 in ~12s); the slowest is C13 (4 stingers across ~39s including the "Heto na!" chorus) and C1 (~31s for 2 items + tease block). `DIRECTLY OBSERVED` (calculated from script timestamps).

`ANALYST INFERENCE:` stingers serve three functions here: **(1) item boundary** — they tell the listener "new headline" without the anchor having to say it; **(2) energy pacing** — drum/brass accents re-energize every 3–6 seconds, matching the broadcast's ~5-minute sprint; **(3) genre labeling** — sports/showbiz accents (C2, C5) pre-announce a category shift before the anchor speaks it. The failure mode is visible in C18's density: 3.3s per item leaves no room for a frame line or breathing pause — the rundown feels like a machine-gun list (`ANALYST INFERENCE` from the ~10s block containing 3 beeps + 3 headlines + 2-part headline).

## 8. Reusable Headline Formulas (Abstract — No Copied Lines)

The following are abstracted templates distilled from §3–§6. They are safe to reuse because they encode *structure*, not wording.

### Formula 1 — The State-Change Headline (workhorse; ~60% of corpus headlines)
```
[TOPIC NOUN PHRASE] , [PASSIVE RESULT VERB / RESULT STATE] !
```
Examples-in-pattern (not copied): "ATM scammer, arestado!" · "Cyanide bottles, nakumpiska!" · "Fujairah Open, naudlot!"
- Why it works (`ANALYST INFERENCE`): front-loads the topic, states the change in ≤4 words, one breath, lands on a stressed result word.
- **Use for:** local and national hard news (reveal mode).

### Formula 2 — The Cause-Alarm Headline (for crisis stories)
```
[HEAT/WARNING ADJECTIVE] + [TOPIC] + dahil sa [CAUSE] !
```
Pattern-evidence: C1 ("mainit-init ang tensyon… dahil sa…"), C14 ("…nananatiling mataas dahil sa giyera sa Middle East!"), C5 ("…dahil sa patuloy na hidwaan sa Middle East"). `CROSS-SCRIPT PATTERN` (C1, C5, C14, C16, C17 use "dahil sa" consequence clauses inside headlines).
- **Use for:** the lead national item; the cause clause supplies context in one breath so the story block can start at the event.

### Formula 3 — The Curiosity Tease (soft-category slot)
```
[QUESTION WORD] + [SUBJECT] + [kaya?]    (no outcome stated)
```
Pattern-evidence: C5 ("sino-sinong artist… ang waging sa Oscars 2026?"), C13 ("Anong bagong aksyon ng pamahalaan…?"), C16 mid-break ("Ano-anong films kaya…?"). `CROSS-SCRIPT PATTERN` (C5, C13, C16).
- **Use for:** sports and showbiz only, and only when the story airs after the break.

### Formula 4 — The Directive Headline (imperative tease)
```
[IMPERATIVE: Alamin / Tutukan / Abangan / Mag-ingat] + [OBJECT]
```
Pattern-evidence: C1 ("Mag-ingat po tayo…"), C13 ("alamin na", "tutukan"), C7/C14 ("abangan"), C16 ("Tutok lang…"). `CROSS-SCRIPT PATTERN` (C1, C7, C13, C14, C16).
- **Use for:** follow-up items or as the closing item of the rundown; pairs well with Formula 3.

### Formula 5 — The Compound Headline (two facts, one slot; density move)
```
[EVENT RESULT] ! [NAMED PERSON / AFFECTED PARTY] , [STATE] !
```
Pattern-evidence: C18 ("ATP Challenger 50 Fujairah Open, naudlot! Pilipinong tennis player Francis Alcantara, hindi natuloy!"); C5's sports item (event + "Pilipino tennis player…"); C4's showbiz item (name + verdict).
- **Use for:** sports (event + Filipino athlete) — the only slot where the corpus consistently pairs a macro event with a named human; gives listeners a face to care about.

### Formula 6 — The Rundown Architecture (the block itself)
```
[FRAME LINE] + (HEADLINE + STINGER) × 2–4 + [HANDOFF LINE]
```
Pattern-evidence: C4, C5, C10, C16, C17 (frame + handoff present); C1 (frame as question + extended "tumutok" handoff). `CROSS-SCRIPT PATTERN`.
- Rules from the corpus: 2 items minimum, 4 items maximum; alternate anchors; hard news first, soft last; per-item separator (beep = neutral, accent = energy, genre accent = label); handoff line announces the start of the story block.

## 9. Weak Headline Sets (Critical Review)

Verdicts below are `ANALYST INFERENCE` built on `DIRECTLY OBSERVED` text; flag that C2/C9/C12/C15 garbling may be transcription loss, not authoring failure.

1. **C2 — fragments posing as headlines.** Items like "Balitang sports." and "At impormasyon." name a category, not a story. Even allowing heavy transcription corruption, the intended structure appears to be *category labels*, which cannot function as teasers. The block also lands **before** the station ID with no frame line, which reads as accidental inversion rather than the deliberate hook C13 achieves with the same order.
2. **C6 — same story twice in one rundown.** The WPS cyanide story is item 1 ("Sampung bote ng cyanide… nasabat ng PCG") and item 3 ("Natagpuan… ang Chinese Coast Guard na nagbubuhos di-umano ng cyanide sa Spratly Islands at Ayungin Shoal") — the same fact set, two slots. With only 3 items total, the rundown wastes its scarcest resource; the international oil story gets no standalone item despite being the third segment of the broadcast.
3. **C14 — content-free tease.** Item 3 ("At highlights na resulta ng Academy Awards ngayong taon!") tells the listener nothing: no film, no actor, no outcome, no question to create curiosity. It is a placeholder, and it occupies the slot where the corpus's best entries put a named tease (C5) or a verdict (C4).
4. **C17 — over-long, under-covered.** Both headlines run ~18–20 words (≈8–10 seconds each), and neither covers sports or showbiz even though both air in this broadcast. The block thus fails both compression and coverage.
5. **C15 — rushed and thin.** 2 items in ~10 seconds with one garbled item ("…arestado dahil sa financial accounts kami"). The block is the shortest in the corpus; no sports/showbiz/sports tease despite a dedicated "Special Updates" stinger available as branding.
6. **C9/C12 — clarity failures.** C9's "At BNP." (unexpanded acronym) and C12's "Iska-murder" would confuse a listener even if delivery were clean; the rundown's job is instant comprehension.
7. **C18 — density without air.** 3 beeps + 3 headlines (one compound) in ~10 seconds leaves zero pause; the listener cannot register item 1 before item 2 arrives. The fastest block is also the least memorable (`ANALYST INFERENCE`).

**Strong counter-examples:** C4 (4 items × 6–8 words × 4 stingers, exclamatory, full category coverage, clean handoff) and C5 (mixed reveal/tease with thematic frame "Una sa ating ruta") are the most reusable models in the corpus.

## 10. Reusable Principles (KEEP / ADAPT / DO NOT COPY)

### KEEP AS A PRINCIPLE
- **One fact per headline, result-state verb, 8–14 words** (Corpus mode across 18 entries).
- **2–4 items; hard news first, soft categories last; alternate anchors.**
- **Per-item separator at 3–6s spacing** (beep for neutrality, accent for energy, genre accent for sports/showbiz).
- **Reveal hard news, tease soft news** — and reserve question-form for the soft slots.
- **Frame line + handoff line** around the block so the list reads as a program.
- **Compound headline for sports** (event + named Filipino athlete).

### ADAPT CAREFULLY
- **Headlines before station ID** (C2, C8, C13): a powerful hook only when preceded by a chorus/attention line (C13's "Heto na!" — adapt the *idea of a hook*, not the chant).
- **Audience-address tease block** (C1: enumerating the listener's own situations before "tumutok na") — strong but only if kept ≤3 lines.
- **Pre-break second tease layer** naming the post-break sports/showbiz story (11/18 entries) — pair with a "magbabalik" bumper.
- **Category-identified stingers in the rundown** (C2, C5) — effective, but only 2/18 entries do it; be sure the audio operator can hit them.

### DO NOT COPY
- Any station-specific slogan, chant, or station ID tied to a callsign/frequency ("Heto na! Heto na!" chorus; "Tatak-totoo!"; any 91.26-style invented frequency).
- Any entry's exact headline wording, sign-off formulas, or segment names ("Special Updates" stinger phrase, "Buzzing Showbiz", "Balitang Nasyonal" — these are identity markers, not techniques).
- Thematic frames that belong to one team's concept (e.g., C5's travel/route vocabulary for headlines — "ruta", "kabiyahero" — is that team's creative property).
